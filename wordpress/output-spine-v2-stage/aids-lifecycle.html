The Ratings

Course Profile
Logistics 5/5

Thirty years of operational refinement created what might be the most impressive mobile logistics operation in cycling. Over 600 roadie volunteers wake before dawn and work past dark to move an entire camp city — tents, showers, food service, medical facilities, bike tech — 545 miles down the California coast. Rest stops every 15-20 miles provide water, electrolytes, snacks, and medical support. Boudin caters full lunches. SAG buses and 20-30 sweep vehicles ensure no rider is ever stranded. Bike tech support is on-course and at every camp. Chiropractic care is available at lunch. This is not a race with aid stations — this is a traveling village with full infrastructure.

Editorial
Prestige 4/5

AIDS/LifeCycle and its predecessor the California AIDS Ride have been operating since 1994, raising over $300 million for HIV/AIDS services. It is the largest HIV/AIDS fundraising event in the world. Media coverage spans national outlets, with the final 2025 ride drawing attention from the San Francisco Chronicle, ABC News, and national LGBTQ+ press. Completing the 545 is a genuine achievement that earns deep respect in cycling and philanthropic communities. It is not a race with competitive prestige — it is something arguably more meaningful.

Facts & History

The California AIDS Ride launched in 1994 as a week-long cycling event from San Francisco to Los Angeles to raise money and awareness for HIV/AIDS services. After Pallotta Teamworks faced controversy over overhead costs, the event was restructured in 2002 as AIDS/LifeCycle, directly co-produced by San Francisco AIDS Foundation and Los Angeles LGBT Center. Over 30 years, the event raised more than $300 million and became the largest HIV/AIDS fundraiser in the world. In September 2024, the organizations announced that the 2025 ride would be the final AIDS/LifeCycle, citing rising production costs and declining post-pandemic participation. The final ride on June 1-7, 2025 drew 2,067 cyclists and 750+ roadies. SFAF launched Cycle to Zero as a 3-day successor event for 2026. Founded in 1994. By Pallotta Teamworks (as California AIDS Ride, 1994-2002). Relaunched as AIDS/LifeCycle in 2002 by Kevin Honeycutt, co-produced by San Francisco AIDS Foundation and Los Angeles LGBT Center..

Reputation: The most culturally significant cycling event in American history. Not just a ride but a movement — a 30-year tradition of LGBTQ+ community, activism, remembrance, and defiance.

The Course

Seven days of California coastal beauty and emotional intensity. The route traverses the full diversity of the state's Central Coast — from San Francisco fog through redwood forests, over mountain ridges, through agricultural valleys, past wine country, over coastal passes, and along the Pacific Coast Highway to a Santa Monica finish. The terrain is varied but always paved, with significant climbing concentrated on Days 1, 3, 4, and 6. The real challenge is cumulative: 545 miles over 7 consecutive days in the saddle, compounded by camping, emotional weight, and California's variable June weather.

Signature challenge: The cumulative toll of 545 miles over 7 days. No single day is impossible, but the relentless accumulation of miles, climbing, weather, camping, and emotional weight creates an endurance challenge that transforms riders. The body adapts; the spirit grows. By Day 7, riding into Santa Monica feels like crossing into a different version of your life.

MILE
Day 1 — Skyline Boulevard
The ride's hardest climbing day. 96 miles from San Francisco to Santa Cruz with 4,700+ feet of elevation gain. Skyline Boulevard climbs out of the fog and into the redwoods. Burns 3,800+ calories. Welcome to the 545.
196
MILE
Day 3 — The Quadbuster
A 15% grade hill that earns its name. 75 miles through rolling terrain into Paso Robles with 1,800+ feet of climbing. Can be brutally hot. The second hilliest day of the ride.
275
MILE
Day 4 — Evil Twins
Two (or three, depending on who you ask) steep peaks that mark the halfway point. 88 miles from Paso Robles to Santa Maria. Emotionally and physically the midpoint crucible. The halfway celebration at the top is unforgettable.
430
MILE
Day 6 — Gaviota Pass
1,100 feet of climbing over the pass before dropping to the Santa Barbara coast. 84 miles from Lompoc to Ventura. The last major climbing day before the coastal finish.

Race Logistics

Airport
SFO (San Francisco International) for start. LAX (Los Angeles International) for finish. Many participants fly one direction and have bikes shipped.
Lodging
Camp cities are provided each night at designated locations: Harvey West Park (Santa Cruz), San Lorenzo County Park (King City), Mid-State Fairgrounds (Paso Robles), Preisker Park (Santa Maria), River Bend Park (Lompoc), San Buenaventura State Beach (Ventura). Tents provided or bring your own. Some veterans self-support in hotels along the route. Gear trucks transport personal bags between camps.
Food
All meals included. Breakfast and dinner at camp. Lunch provided by Boudin at midday stops. Rest stop snacks and hydration every 15-20 miles. Vegetarian and omnivorous options available. You will not go hungry.
Packet Pickup
Orientation Day at the Cow Palace (Daly City) the day before ride start. Mandatory check-in for all participants. Bike check, gear drop, and opening ceremony.
Parking
Cow Palace parking available for Orientation Day. Most participants arrange transportation to/from start and finish. Charter bus and shuttle options organized by the event.
RIDERS REPORT
Set small goals on the way to the big goal to get to the finish line. Use mental toughness and focus on accomplishing tasks step by step.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at AIDS/LifeCycle?

+

California's coast in June serves every weather condition the state has. Morning departures in San Francisco fog (55F) give way to inland heat reaching 95F in Paso Robles by midday. Coastal winds are persistent, particularly afternoon headwinds. One rider described Day 3 as 'brutally hot and hilly' with inland temperatures soaring. Yet evening camps drop to 50-55F, and the coastal days near Ventura and Santa Barbara deliver that perfect 65-75F with ocean breezes. The variety is part of the adventure — but the inland heat days require serious hydration discipline.

How are the logistics for AIDS/LifeCycle?

+

How adventurous is AIDS/LifeCycle?

+

Riding 545 miles down the California coast over 7 days is an adventure by any measure. The route passes through redwood forests, over mountain ridges, through agricultural valleys, past Hearst Castle, over Gaviota Pass, along the Santa Barbara coastline, and finishes on the Pacific Coast Highway into Santa Monica. Each night is a camp city in a new town — Santa Cruz, King City, Paso Robles, Santa Maria, Lompoc, Ventura. The journey from fog-shrouded San Francisco to sun-drenched Los Angeles traverses the full diversity of California's Central Coast. What elevates it beyond a scenic tour is the emotional dimension — riding past memorial panels, hearing names read aloud, feeling the weight of the mission in every pedal stroke.

Should I race AIDS/LifeCycle?

+

AIDS/LifeCycle ended in 2025 after 30 years and $300 million raised. If you rode it, you know what it meant. If you never did, the successor event Cycle to Zero (3-day, SFAF-produced) launches in May 2026. The 545-mile journey from San Francisco to Los Angeles is gone, but the community and mission endure. This was never about the miles — it was about why you rode them.
