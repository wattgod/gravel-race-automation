Facts & History

Around the Bay in a Day was born on Sunday 17 October 1993, created by Bicycle Victoria (now Bicycle Network) following the success of their Great Victorian Bike Ride multi-day touring event. The concept was simple and audacious: ride around Port Phillip Bay in a single day. The inaugural event attracted 2,700 registered riders (with another 1,000 turned away due to ferry capacity), starting at 5:30 am from JL Murphy Reserve in Port Melbourne. Despite the long distance and many riders finishing in the dark, it was deemed an overwhelming success. The event grew rapidly — 8,600 riders in 2004, nearly 11,000 in 2005, over 14,000 in 2006, and a peak of more than 16,000 in 2008. What began as a cycling event became a Melbourne institution, known as 'the ride that stops Melbourne.' Multiple distance options were added over the years, a partnership with The Smith Family charity was established, and title sponsors including Bupa and United Energy brought the event to a mainstream audience far beyond the cycling community. Founded in 1993. By Bicycle Victoria (now Bicycle Network).

Reputation: Australia's biggest one-day cycling event and a Melbourne cultural institution. The ride that stops a city of five million people for a Sunday in October. Where 10,000 riders prove that cycling is not a sport, it is a community.

1993: Inaugural event — 2,700 riders depart JL Murphy Reserve, Port Melbourne at 5:30 am. Another 1,000 turned away due to ferry capacity. Many finish in the dark.
2004: Over 8,600 riders participate as the event establishes itself as Australia's biggest one-day ride
2005: Nearly 11,000 riders register, raising $300,000 for The Smith Family charity
2006: Total entries exceed 14,000, raising $440,000 for charity
2008: Peak participation of over 16,000 riders — the largest single-day cycling event in Australian history
Multiple distance options introduced over the years, growing from the original 210 km to six routes (20 km to 300 km)
2024: 30th edition of the event — three decades of continuous operation as a Melbourne institution

The Course

A flat, coastal circumnavigation of Port Phillip Bay on sealed roads and paths, punctuated by a 40-minute ferry crossing between Queenscliff and Sorrento. The Classic route runs anti-clockwise: west from Albert Park over the West Gate Bridge, through Werribee and Little River to Geelong, along the Bellarine Peninsula to Queenscliff, across the water by ferry to Sorrento, then back along the Mornington Peninsula and Beach Road to the finish at Albert Park. The terrain is relentlessly flat — the challenge is distance, wind, and endurance rather than gradient. The scenery shifts from urban Melbourne to industrial western suburbs, open farmland, Geelong's waterfront, the coastal Bellarine Peninsula, and finally the iconic Beach Road run home.

Signature challenge: The sheer distance — 210 km of flat coastal riding with no hills to break the monotony and no shelter from the wind. The western shore between Werribee and Geelong is where Around the Bay reveals itself: in a headwind, this section is a war of attrition. The ferry provides a 40-minute reprieve, but you still have 80 km to ride on the other side.

MILE
The Pre-Dawn Start
Rolling out of Albert Park in the dark with thousands of riders. Red tail lights stretching ahead like a river of stars. Cool spring air at 8-12C — arm warmers and a vest mandatory. The nervous energy of 10,000 people about to ride 210 km carries you through the first kilometres. Over the West Gate Bridge — a freeway bridge normally forbidden to cyclists, opened for you, with the Melbourne skyline glowing behind and Port Phillip Bay stretching into the darkness ahead.
25
MILE
The Western Grind
The least glamorous section of the route. Flat, exposed roads through Werribee and Little River, with the You Yangs visible to the west and Port Phillip Bay to the east. This is where the wind lives. In a good year, it is a tailwind and you sail. In a bad year, it is a headwind and you suffer. Drafting matters here. Stay in a group. The road is straight, the terrain is flat, and there is nothing to do but pedal and manage your effort for the 180 km still ahead.
55
MILE
Geelong and the Bellarine
Geelong's waterfront is a welcome break from the exposed western plains. The route picks up the Bellarine Peninsula, rolling through Portarlington and along the coast toward Queenscliff. The scenery improves markedly — coastal views, small towns, and the sense that the ferry is approaching. Queenscliff is the halfway psychological landmark. The rest stop here is a hive of activity as riders prepare for the ferry crossing.
80
MILE
The Ferry
The signature moment of Around the Bay. Bikes stacked on the deck, riders stretching their legs, the Heads of Port Phillip Bay — the narrow gap between Point Nepean and Point Lonsdale — passing below. Forty minutes of recovery that no other cycling event offers. The ferry is both practical (it cuts off the inaccessible southern tip of the bay) and experiential (it transforms a road ride into a journey). Sorrento appears on the other side. You are now on the Mornington Peninsula. The second half begins.
95
MILE
The Mornington Peninsula
From Sorrento down through Safety Beach, Dromana, and along the coast of the Mornington Peninsula. The afternoon sun is on the bay. The legs are starting to feel 150+ km of riding. The terrain remains flat but the accumulated distance is now the challenge. Rest stops on the peninsula keep nutrition topped up. The field thins as faster riders pull ahead and slower riders settle into survival mode.
115
MILE
Beach Road — The Run Home
The most famous stretch of recreational cycling road in Australia. Beach Road from Mordialloc to St Kilda is where Melbourne's cycling culture was born — and it is where Around the Bay brings you home. The bay is on your left, the afternoon light is golden, and you are riding the final 20 km of a 210 km journey with the finish at Albert Park ahead. The fatigue is real. The satisfaction is greater. The event village at Albert Park is a celebration of completion, not competition.
RIDERS REPORT
50k headwind section home
The final 50 kilometers feature brutal headwinds. One rider described it as the windiest day in Melbourne for 5 years, with conditions so severe that people struggled to stand up. The wind along Beach Road was described as borderline dangerous.
Ferry crossing transition
At the halfway point riders board a ferry. The key challenge is staying moving while on the ferry to avoid seizing up, as riders still have another 100km to go after the ferry crossing.
The first 100km features favorable tailwind conditions with long trains of riders forming naturally. Rolling hills are present on approach roads like Q Boulevard. The return journey includes familiar coastline roads on the left side.

Race Logistics

Airport
Melbourne Tullamarine Airport (MEL) — major international hub with direct flights from all Australian capitals plus nonstop services from Asia, Middle East, and North America. 25 km from Albert Park, 30-45 minutes by taxi or SkyBus to Southern Cross Station then tram.
Lodging
Albert Park, St Kilda, and South Melbourne are all within cycling distance of the start and offer abundant accommodation. Hotels near Albert Park range from $150-300 AUD/night on event weekend. St Kilda has backpackers, mid-range hotels, and Airbnb options along the foreshore. The CBD is 3 km away with the full spectrum of Melbourne hotels. Book early — 10,000+ riders means nearby accommodation fills fast, particularly the Pullman Melbourne Albert Park and Mercure Melbourne Albert Park.
Food
Melbourne is one of the world's great food cities. Pre-ride carb loading options are endless — Italian on Lygon Street, Asian on Victoria Street, or any of the hundreds of restaurants in the CBD and inner suburbs. Post-ride, the Albert Park event village has food trucks and the finish-line celebration. St Kilda's Acland Street and Fitzroy Street offer everything from fish and chips to fine dining within walking distance of the event precinct.
Packet Pickup
Event weekend at Albert Park event village. Check Bicycle Network website for specific times. Ride kits include your numbered jersey, timing tag (for app tracking, not competition), ferry allocation sticker, and event guide.
Parking
Albert Park has limited parking — expect road closures and heavy demand on event morning. Public transport (trams to Albert Park / St Kilda Road) is strongly recommended. If driving, arrive very early or park in the CBD and tram to the start.
RIDERS REPORT
Consider riding to the start line instead of driving - it can be faster than finding parking and provides a good tailwind warmup over the Westgate Bridge.
Start eating every half hour after the first hectic hour. The first hour involves weaving through traffic, so stay hydrated but don't worry about eating until things settle down.
Stay moving while on the ferry at the halfway point to avoid seizing up, as you still have 100km remaining after the crossing.
Line up at the back of the first wave behind the Bicycle Network riders for better organization and flow.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at United Energy Around the Bay?

+

Melbourne in October is mild but unpredictable. Average temperatures of 11-21C are comfortable for cycling, but the city's 'four seasons in one day' reputation exists for a reason. You can leave Albert Park in sunshine and hit rain at Geelong. The primary weather challenge is wind — the western shore between Werribee and Geelong is exposed, and crosswinds off Port Phillip Bay can turn a flat road into a grind. The Bellarine Peninsula is similarly wind-prone. The climate is not extreme in either direction — it does not bake you or freeze you — which keeps this score low. Bring layers, bring a rain jacket, and accept that Melbourne will do what Melbourne does.

Road closures, ferry coordination, rest stops, and SAG support all run with the precision of an organization that has done this more times than most events exist.

How adventurous is United Energy Around the Bay?

+

The ferry crossing from Queenscliff to Sorrento is genuinely unique — a 40-minute boat ride across the mouth of Port Phillip Bay, bikes stacked on the deck, surrounded by other riders. It breaks up the riding and adds a maritime dimension that no other cycling event offers at this scale. The circumnavigation concept is inherently satisfying — you ride around an entire body of water and return to where you started. Melbourne's Beach Road, the You Yangs on the horizon, the Bellarine coastline, and the Mornington Peninsula all deliver scenic variety. But this is a coastal road ride through suburbs and farmland, not a wilderness expedition. The adventure is in the scale and the journey, not the terrain.

Should I race United Energy Around the Bay?

+

Yes if you want to experience Australia's largest cycling event in one of the world's great cities. Yes if you value community over competition and want a well-supported 210 km endurance challenge on flat terrain. Skip if you need climbing, technical terrain, or a competitive racing environment.
