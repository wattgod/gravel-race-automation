Facts & History

Created to be among the world's most challenging self-supported gravel events, showcasing Andalusia's extreme terrain from desert to alpine. Quickly earned reputation as the hardest gravel race on earth. Founded in 2018. By Transiberica Ultracycling.

Reputation: The world's hardest gravel race—where ultra-endurance meets extreme conditions.

Established as benchmark for ultra-endurance gravel difficulty
Growing international field from ultra-endurance community
Sub-48 hour finishes by elite riders
[03]

The Course

An 800km expedition through Andalusia's extremes. Desert badlands that look like Mars, Europe's only true desert (Tabernas), and the 3,396m Pico Veleta climb. Self-supported means you carry what you need between remote villages. The combination of distance, elevation, heat, and self-reliance is unmatched in gravel racing.

Signature challenge: The complete package—800km of desert heat, alpine altitude, and multi-day self-supported suffering through some of Europe's most extreme terrain.

Climb after Tabernas MI 217
14km climb with 1,100m elevation gain that comes after 350km of riding. Riders report knee pain starting on this climb.
First 300km
Basically one big climb with first 1,000 meters of climbing in just 28 kilometers. Sets the tone for massive elevation early in the race.
Coast to mountain transition
After reaching the coast, riders face 3 hours of climbing up a rock garden to get back up from sea level.
Night riding at altitude
Mental battle when it's dark and you can't see what's coming. Riders report nearly falling asleep and having to rest at 2,000m elevation where it's freezing.

RACE-SPECIFIC: BADLANDS
HEAT PREP August in Granada, Andalusia, Spain — August in Andalusia is brutal. Desert sections reach 40-45°C (104-113°F). Pico Veleta at 3,396m can drop to 5-10°C at night with potential freezing. 30-40°C temperature swings in a single day. Afternoon thunderstorms in mountains possible. Begin heat acclimatization 10–14 days before Badlands — 20–30min sauna sessions or midday rides in full kit. Pre-load sodium 48 hours out. Target 500–750ml/hr with electrolytes on race day. Race-day realities: Extreme heat in desert (40°C+).
NUTRITION Ultra-distance fueling for 497 miles: 80–100g carbs/hour from mile 1 — don’t wait until you’re hungry. Practice your exact race-day nutrition in every long training ride. Carry backup calories. 497 miles burns 8,000–12,000+ calories — you cannot replace them all, but you must try.
ALTITUDE Badlands with 52,493ft of climbing, much of it above 8,000ft demands altitude preparation. Arrive 5–7 days early for acclimatization. Expect 5–15% power reduction at altitude. Increase iron intake 4 weeks out. Hydrate aggressively — altitude increases fluid loss by 20–40%. Alpine cold at altitude.
TERRAIN Highly technical terrain at Badlands demands practice on similar surfaces. Ride desert gravel, alpine passes, technical trails at race-day cadence weekly. Expect: desert gravel, alpine passes, technical mtb trails. Practice cornering, descending, and power delivery on unstable surfaces. Dial in tire pressure before race week — 5 PSI wrong costs you 15+ minutes over 497 miles.

Race Logistics

Airport
AGP (Málaga) - 90min drive; GRX (Granada) - local but limited; SVQ (Seville) - 2.5hr
Lodging
Granada city for pre/post race. During race: bivvy/sleeping bag as needed, occasional hotel/hostel stops for resupply and rest. Plan your sleep strategy.
Food
Self-supported—resupply at villages, bars, shops. Carry enough between stops. Spanish villages may have limited hours.
Packet Pickup
Pre-race in Granada with bike check and mandatory equipment verification.
Parking
Granada start/finish area.
RIDERS REPORT
Start deliberately slow, keep heart rate down, and ease into it since it's such a long day. Resist urge to go faster early on.
Plan nutrition strategy with 90g carbs per hour for first part, then settle into sustainable pacing. Carry enough to be self-sufficient for first 24 hours.
When falling asleep while riding, take a 30-minute rest even in poor conditions rather than risk crashing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at Badlands?

+

Cara Dixon reports 42°C desert temperatures nearly caused heatstroke: "Just before the climb out of the desert I had to lie down, I knew my body well enough to know that I was balancing on the edge of heat stroke." Starting at cool morning temps, the race transitions to brutal afternoon heat where riders practice "vampire racing" - hiding from sun during peak hours.

How are the logistics for Badlands?

+

Registration opens for exactly 5 days in November with demand exceeding spots 5:1. Granada provides excellent access via multiple airports, but self-supported format means managing 100km+ desert stretches with zero resupply. "The event organisers had agreed with a cafe in Gor to open for 24 hours for the riders, which proved to be invaluable," Bradley notes about the strategic support points.

How adventurous is Badlands?

+

Chris Hall calls it "so much more than just a bike race. It's an experience with an incredible community exploring one of the most beautiful and brutal parts of Southern Spain." The route crosses three distinct desert systems, volcanic coastlines, and UNESCO Biosphere Reserves. "The views never get old," Hall notes about returning annually to the same otherworldly landscapes.

How prestigious is Badlands?

+

Elite riders like Mattia de Marchi, Sarah Scozzaro, and James Mark Hayden validate the field depth. "Even after riding for close to 750 km and without any sleep for the final 48 hours, I don't remember any moments of panic," de Marchi reports. The event attracts serious ultra-endurance specialists who treat it as a career-defining challenge rather than weekend adventure.

Should I race Badlands?

+

Yes if you have genuine ultra-endurance experience and want the ultimate test. Yes if you can handle extreme heat and self-support logistics. Skip if you haven't completed multi-day bikepacking events or can't adapt to 40°C+ conditions.
