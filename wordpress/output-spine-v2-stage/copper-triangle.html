Facts & History

The Copper Triangle was conceived to make the most of Copper Mountain's unique position at the intersection of three Colorado mountain passes. The route — a 79-mile loop over Fremont Pass, Tennessee Pass, and Vail Pass — had long been a favorite of local cyclists, and Olmsted formalized it into an organized event in 2006 with the Davis Phinney Foundation as the beneficiary. Davis Phinney, the Boulder-born professional cyclist diagnosed with Parkinson's disease at age 40, lent both his name and his presence to the event. The ride grew from its first edition to 2,500 participants under the Roll Massif banner, then moved to Outside Events in 2022, and was acquired by The Ride Collective in 2023. Twenty years and over $1.5 million in charitable donations later, the Copper Triangle is Colorado's signature high-altitude loop ride. Founded in 2006. By Scott Olmsted, co-founder, in partnership with Copper Mountain Resort and the Davis Phinney Foundation.

Reputation: Colorado's premier high-altitude loop ride. The three-pass circuit that Front Range cyclists treat as a summer rite of passage — and visiting riders treat as an altitude reality check.

2006: Inaugural Copper Triangle ride from Copper Mountain, benefiting the Davis Phinney Foundation for Parkinson's
2015: 10th anniversary edition — cumulative fundraising crosses the $1 million mark for the Davis Phinney Foundation
Since 2006, over $1.5 million raised for Parkinson's research, averaging roughly $140,000 per year
2022: 16th annual edition draws over 2,000 riders; event joins the Outside Events Cycling Series
2023: The Ride Collective acquires Outside Cycling Events, bringing the Copper Triangle into a portfolio of eight professional rides across Colorado and New Mexico

The Course

A three-act alpine loop that never lets you forget where you are. The Copper Triangle traces a triangle through the heart of the Colorado Rockies, connecting three mountain passes through three distinct valleys. You climb past industrial mining heritage on Fremont, descend into America's highest city at Leadville, ascend through WWII military history at Camp Hale, and close the loop on the car-free Vail Pass bike path. The entire route sits above 9,000 feet — there is no low-altitude recovery. The grades are moderate, the road surfaces are good, and the scenery is relentless. What makes this ride hard is not the climbing profile on paper. It's the thin air that makes every gradient feel steeper than it reads.

Signature challenge: The cumulative altitude. No single pass is a leg-breaker. The grades are moderate, the distances between passes are manageable. But the Copper Triangle never lets you below 9,000 feet. By the time you're climbing Vail Pass — your third summit above 10,000 feet — the oxygen deficit has been compounding for 65 miles. The thin air is the opponent, not the gradient.

MILE
Copper Mountain — The Dawn Start
Rolling out of Burning Stones Plaza at 9,712 feet in the cool mountain dawn. The parking lot is buzzing with 2,500 riders. You turn south onto CO-91 and begin climbing immediately. The Tenmile Range towers to the east. Your lungs register the altitude before your legs do.
13
MILE
Fremont Pass — 11,318 ft
The high point of the day. CO-91 climbs steadily at 2.5-3% past the Climax Mine — the terraced slopes of what was once the world's largest molybdenum mine spread across the mountainside. Mining trucks share the highway. The air gets thin. At 11,318 feet you crest Fremont Pass and the Arkansas River valley opens below, with Leadville's grid visible in the distance. First pass done.
25
MILE
Leadville — America's Highest City
You descend into the high valley and roll through Leadville at 10,152 feet — the highest incorporated city in America. Victorian-era mining architecture lines Harrison Avenue. The aid station here is a welcome stop. Refuel well — Tennessee Pass and Battle Mountain await.
40
MILE
Tennessee Pass — 10,424 ft
Heading northwest on US-24, you pass the ruins of Camp Hale — where the Army's 10th Mountain Division trained for alpine warfare in WWII. The ghosts of those soldiers are palpable in the valley. A moderate 4-mile climb with switchbacks brings you over Tennessee Pass at 10,424 feet, with a memorial to the 10th Mountain Division at the summit. The descent through forest, streams, and meadows toward Minturn is one of the ride's great rewards.
50
MILE
Battle Mountain — The Unofficial Fourth Pass
Between Tennessee Pass and Minturn, Battle Mountain is the pass nobody warns you about. A rolling climb through dense forest that on fresh legs would barely register. On legs that have already summited two passes above 10,000 feet, every false flat feels like a wall. The descent into Minturn is the release.
58
MILE
Minturn — The Quiet Valley Town
You drop into Minturn, a small working town in the Vail Valley that the ski tourists never see. The Eagle River runs through town. An aid station provides refueling before the final pass. From here the route picks up the frontage road and bike path east toward Vail.
68
MILE
Vail Pass — 10,662 ft
The final pass. The route transitions to the dedicated Vail Pass Recreation Path — no cars, no trucks, just you and the bike path climbing through the forest. The eastern approach from the Vail side is about 15 miles with steady 3-4% grades and entertaining switchbacks near the top. At 10,662 feet you crest Vail Pass. The Gore Range fills the skyline. Two wheels, three passes, done.
74
MILE
The Return — Descent to Copper Mountain
Five miles of downhill on the bike path from Vail Pass back to Copper Mountain. The Tenmile Range ahead, the wind in your face, the Alpine Celebration waiting at Burning Stones Plaza. Beer, food, music, and the quiet satisfaction of a three-pass loop at altitude. You're done.

Race Logistics

Airport
Denver International (DEN) — 90 minutes to Copper Mountain via I-70 West. Straightforward mountain highway access. Eagle County Airport (EGE) is 60 minutes west but has limited service.
Lodging
Copper Mountain Resort is the most convenient option — walk from your condo to the start line at Burning Stones Plaza. Book early: August is peak season. Frisco (10 minutes north on I-70) has more hotel variety and lower prices. Silverthorne and Dillon also offer Summit County lodging. Leadville (on the route, 30 minutes south) has historic hotels and motels at mountain-town prices.
Food
Five aid stations along the course provide hydration, fruit, and snacks. Alpine Celebration at the finish includes lunch and beer garden. Copper Mountain has resort restaurants. Frisco has a walkable main street with restaurants and a brewery. Leadville's Harrison Avenue has restaurants if you're staying the night before.
Packet Pickup
Burning Stones Plaza, Copper Mountain Resort. Pre-ride expo and pickup. Details published by The Ride Collective before the event.
Parking
Copper Mountain Resort parking areas. Arrive early — 2,500 riders fill the lots. The loop format means your car is waiting at the finish.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at Copper Triangle — Colorado's Three-Pass Alpine Loop?

+

August in the Colorado high country is generally gorgeous — clear blue mornings, spectacular visibility, comfortable riding temperatures. But the afternoon thunderstorm risk is real and repeats almost daily. Lightning above 10,000 feet is not theoretical. The early 6:00 AM start is designed to get riders over the passes and back to Copper before the storms build around 1:00-2:00 PM. Start on time and ride with purpose, and you'll have a spectacular weather day. Dally past noon on the final pass, and the mountains will remind you who's in charge.

+

Organization has been refined across 20 editions.

How adventurous is Copper Triangle — Colorado's Three-Pass Alpine Loop?

+

Three mountain passes through three distinct Colorado valleys create a genuine journey. You ride past the Climax Mine on Fremont Pass — once the world's largest molybdenum mine, its terraced slopes visible for miles. You descend into Leadville at 10,152 feet, America's highest incorporated city, where Victorian mining-era architecture lines the main street. You pass the ruins of Camp Hale where the Army's 10th Mountain Division trained for WWII alpine warfare. You roll through tiny Minturn, a working Vail Valley town that the tourists bypass. The final climb over Vail Pass on the car-free bike path delivers panoramic views of the Gore Range. This is not wilderness bikepacking, but it's a complete Colorado experience.

How prestigious is Copper Triangle — Colorado's Three-Pass Alpine Loop?

+

The Copper Triangle is a well-known Colorado ride with 20 years of history and strong regional recognition. It's on the bucket list for Front Range cyclists. The Davis Phinney Foundation connection adds gravitas — Davis Phinney is a Boulder-born pro cycling legend. But it hasn't reached the national name recognition of the Triple Bypass, Ride the Rockies, or the eastern equivalents. Outside Colorado, most cyclists haven't heard of it. Solid regional prestige, not yet a national cycling institution.

Should I race Copper Triangle — Colorado's Three-Pass Alpine Loop?

+

Yes if you want to experience what genuine high-altitude cycling feels like across three mountain passes in a single day. Yes if you value a well-organized charity ride through Colorado's most historic mountain terrain. Skip if you need race competition or if riding above 10,000 feet is a medical concern.
