The Ratings

Course Profile
Logistics 3/5

This is regional New South Wales at its most remote. Grafton has a small regional airport (Clarence Valley Regional Airport, GFN) with limited domestic services, and Inverell Airport (IVR) runs weekday flights to Brisbane and Sydney. Most riders fly into Coffs Harbour (1.5 hours drive) or Brisbane (5+ hours) and drive to Grafton. You need a car. The point-to-point format complicates logistics further — your car is in Grafton, you finish in Inverell, 228 km away by road. The organisers provide bag transport from Grafton to the Inverell finish, and shuttle arrangements exist, but this is not a start-and-finish-in-the-same-place event. Once you are on-site, the organisation is professional — this is an AusCycling NRS round, part of the ProVelo Super League, with proper timing, marshalling, and road management. The feed station at ~120 km through Glen Innes is well-stocked, and personal feed bags are transported by event vehicles. But getting there and getting home requires planning.

Editorial
Prestige 5/5

This is the most prestigious one-day road race on the Australian domestic calendar, full stop. Running continuously since 1961, the Grafton to Inverell has 65 years of unbroken history. It was run as a UCI international event in 1982, 1984, 1985, 1987, 1988, and 1989 — during which era international teams from New Zealand, Great Britain, and Europe competed for the title. The roll call of winners includes riders who went on to compete in the Olympics and the Tour de France. Paul Curran (GB) won in 1985 and took the 1986 Commonwealth Games road race. David McKenzie, Greg Henderson, and Brodie Chapman are among the names etched into the honour board. Today it is a round of the AusCycling National Road Series and part of the ProVelo Super League. In Australian cycling, 'The Grafton' is a proper noun. Everyone knows what it means.

Facts & History

The Grafton to Inverell Cycle Classic was born in 1961 when Inverell resident Harold Strahley conceived the idea following the opening of the new portion of the Gwydir Highway between Grafton and Glen Innes. The first race attracted 35 riders who had to tackle 110 km of gravel during the 228 km race. A crowd of 6,000 people lined Otho Street in Inverell to welcome the riders — including Australian champion Alan Grindal of Victoria. Like other Australian road races of the era, it began as a handicap event on a course that was largely unsealed gravel. Over the following decades, as the Gwydir Highway was progressively sealed, the race evolved from a bush-road adventure into a professional road classic. The 1980s brought international status — the race was run as a UCI international event in 1982, 1984, 1985, 1987, 1988, and 1989, attracting teams from New Zealand, Great Britain, Sweden, and Switzerland. Harold Strahley, the father of the Grafton to Inverell, died in 1979, but the race he created has endured for 65 years and counting. Founded in 1961. By Harold Strahley (1908-1979), Inverell resident.

Reputation: The Grafton to Inverell is universally regarded as Australia's toughest one-day road race. The 228 km distance, 3,382 m of climbing, and the legendary ascent of Gibraltar Range combine to create a race that has broken riders for over six decades. In Australian cycling, 'The Grafton' is spoken of with the same reverence as European monuments — it is a race that defines careers and tests character. The list of winners reads like a who's who of Australian and international cycling, and the communities of Grafton, Glen Innes, and Inverell remain deeply invested in the event that connects them.

1961: Inaugural race — 35 riders tackle 228 km including 110 km of gravel. 6,000 spectators line Otho Street, Inverell. Australian champion Alan Grindal among the starters.
1982: First UCI international edition — New Zealand Commonwealth Games road team sweep 1st, 2nd, and 3rd.
1985: Paul Curran (Great Britain) wins in 6:00:49, setting a course record that will stand for 26 years. Michael Ansermet (Switzerland) 2nd, Lars Wahlqvist (Sweden) 3rd. Curran goes on to win the 1986 Commonwealth Games road race.
2010: 50th anniversary edition — 344 riders finish from 500+ entries across four divisions.
2011: Mark Jamieson (Tasmania) breaks the 26-year-old course record with 6:00:21 — beating Curran's time by just 28 seconds.
2015: Brodie Chapman competes in the men's race, later becoming one of Australia's most successful women's road professionals.
2024: Event expands to include Pairs Relay and Mt Mitchell to Inverell Open alongside the traditional 228 km classic and women's NRS race.
2025: 64th edition. Tristan Saunders (Team Brennan p/b TP32) wins the men's NRS race. Race moves to March date as part of ProVelo Super League calendar.

The Course

A 228 km point-to-point journey from sea level in the subtropical Clarence Valley to the New England Tablelands at 580 m, crossing the Great Dividing Range via the iconic Gibraltar Range. The course follows the Gwydir Highway westward from Grafton — a two-lane highway that serves as the only direct road link between the coast and the northern tablelands. The character shifts dramatically across the race: flat, humid valley roads give way to the sustained ascent of Gibraltar Range through ancient World Heritage rainforest, then open out onto the rolling, exposed grasslands of the New England Highlands. The final 100 km from Glen Innes to Inverell is deceptively demanding — constant rolling terrain with no flat road and the ever-present threat of headwinds across the open tablelands. Wire Gully at 20 km to go is the tactical crux — the last categorised climb where every decisive move in the race's history has been made. The descent into Inverell is fast, the finish on Vivian Street is decisive, and the applause of Inverell's residents is the reward for 228 km of suffering.

Signature challenge: Gibraltar Range — 16.3 km at 5.7% average gradient through World Heritage-listed Gondwana Rainforest, cresting at approximately 1,100 m with 140 km still remaining. The longest sustained climb in Australian one-day road racing. You cannot win the race on the Range, but you can certainly lose it.

MILE
The Clarence Valley
Rolling out of Grafton at 7:15 AM into the humid subtropical air of the Clarence Valley. The first 25 km are relatively flat — following the Gwydir Highway westward through sugar cane country and river flats. The peloton is large and the pace is controlled. Use this section to settle in, eat early, hydrate aggressively, and stay out of trouble. The humidity is oppressive — 60-80% relative humidity wraps around you immediately. Cattle Creek, the first categorised climb (2.1 km, 5.5%), arrives at 25 km — a sharp ramp that thins the bunch and announces what is coming. Over the top, the road drops into the valley floor before the terrain begins its inexorable upward tilt toward the Range.
16
MILE
Cattle Creek
The first categorised climb, arriving 25 km from the start. At 2.1 km and 5.5% average gradient, Cattle Creek is a sharp jolt to legs that have been spinning on valley roads. It is short enough that strong riders can power over it, but long enough to cause trouble if you go too hard. This is not the climb that decides the race — it is the climb that reminds you what kind of race you are in. Over the top, the road descends briefly before beginning the long approach to Gibraltar Range. The vegetation is already changing — subtropical giving way to eucalyptus forest as the altitude creeps upward.
45
MILE
Gibraltar Range
The defining climb of Australian road cycling. At 72 km from the start, the road tilts upward and does not relent for 16.3 km at 5.7% average gradient. Forty minutes of sustained climbing through World Heritage-listed Gondwana Rainforest — ancient temperate rainforest that has existed since Australia was part of the supercontinent Gondwana. Moss-covered granite boulders, tree ferns filtering dappled light, the air cooling perceptibly as you gain altitude. The gradient is relentless rather than savage — no hairpin switchbacks, no 15% ramps, just an unbroken 5-6% tilt that grinds down your reserves over 16 km. You cannot win the race on the Range, the old saying goes, but you can certainly lose it. Pace is everything. The summit at approximately 1,100 m is not a celebration — it is a waypoint, because you still have 140 km to ride.
75
MILE
The New England Tablelands
Over the summit of Gibraltar Range, the road descends and opens onto the New England Tablelands. The landscape transformation is immediate — from enclosed rainforest to open grassland, granite outcrops, and long views across the highland plateau. The temperature has dropped 6-8C from the Clarence Valley. The terrain is rolling and deceptive — nothing is flat, but nothing is brutally steep either. The constant undulation across 100 km of tableland drains your legs insidiously. Glen Innes arrives at approximately 120 km — the feed station, the psychological halfway point, and the last significant town before the final push to Inverell. Eat everything. Drink everything. Fill your bottles. The next 108 km are on you.
100
MILE
The Long March
From Glen Innes to the Wire Gully approach — roughly 80 km of rolling tableland that tests your mental resilience as much as your legs. The terrain is open and exposed to westerly winds that can be relentless across the highland plateau. Waterloo Range (1.9 km, 5%) provides one more categorised climb to drain whatever reserves you have been hoarding. The road rolls constantly — 580-800 m elevation, never flat, never truly steep, always demanding. This is where amateur riders who went too hard on Gibraltar Range or neglected nutrition pay the price. The final 50 km to Wire Gully are a test of character. The scenery — granite country, cattle stations, the occasional town — is beautiful but barely registers when your legs are screaming.
130
MILE
Wire Gully
The launchpad. Wire Gully (1.3 km, 5%) peaks with approximately 20 km to go, and this is where every decisive move in the race's modern history has been made. In 2025, Tristan Saunders attacked here to take the win. The climb is short and sharp — nothing compared to Gibraltar Range in isolation, but after 200+ km and 3,000+ metres of climbing, 1.3 km at 5% feels like a wall. Over the top, the road turns downhill toward Inverell. If you have survived Wire Gully with the front group, the race is decided in the sprint. If you have been dropped, you ride solo into Inverell with the knowledge that you have just ridden 228 km and climbed 3,382 metres. Either way, you have earned the finish.
140
MILE
The Inverell Descent
Over Wire Gully, the road drops fast toward Inverell. The final 20 km are predominantly downhill — a gift from the terrain after 208 km of suffering. The town appears in the valley below, and the road sweeps down through the outskirts before turning onto Vivian Street for the finish. The sprint, if there is one, happens here — tired riders on depleted legs, trading blows after 6-9 hours in the saddle. The finish line on Vivian Street in Inverell is where 65 years of history converge. You are not the first rider to cross this line in pain, and you will not be the last. But in this moment, you are part of the Grafton to Inverell.

Race Logistics

Airport
Clarence Valley Regional Airport (GFN) in Grafton — limited domestic services. Coffs Harbour Airport (CFS) is the nearest major airport, ~1.5 hours drive north to Grafton. Inverell Airport (IVR) has weekday flights to Brisbane and Sydney. Most riders fly into Coffs Harbour or Brisbane and drive. Sydney to Grafton is ~7 hours by road, Brisbane to Grafton is ~5 hours.
Lodging
Pre-race accommodation in Grafton — motels and Airbnbs from $80-180 AUD per night along the Clarence River. Book early as the town fills for race weekend. Post-race accommodation in Inverell for those staying overnight — similar pricing in this regional gem town. Some riders book accommodation in Glen Innes (midway point) for support crews. The point-to-point format means you need to plan two locations or arrange immediate transport back to Grafton. Carpool arrangements are common within the cycling community.
Food
Pre-race carb-loading in Grafton along the Clarence River restaurant strip — the Rooftop Bar and the Saraton Theatre precinct are local favourites. Regional NSW pub meals are honest and affordable. Post-race in Inverell — the town celebrates the finish with events along Vivian Street. Inverell has a range of cafes and pubs. For race-day nutrition, plan to be self-sufficient between the start and the 120 km feed station, and again for the final 108 km to the finish.
Packet Pickup
Race registration and jersey/number collection at the event headquarters in Grafton on the Friday before the race (opening ceremony Friday evening). Personal feed bags must be labelled and deposited at the registration desk by 6:45 AM Saturday morning for transport to the on-course feed zone. Bag transport to Inverell finish available by arrangement.
Parking
Car parking available at the Grafton start area. For the point-to-point format, many riders arrange car transport — one vehicle at the start, one at the finish, or carpooling with support crews. The event provides bag transport from Grafton to Inverell. Post-race shuttle arrangements from Inverell back to Grafton are available in some years — check the event website.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at Squadron Energy Grafton to Inverell Cycle Classic?

+

The climate challenge is twofold. First, the Clarence Valley start: Grafton in March is warm and humid — 18-22C at dawn, climbing to 28-33C by midday with 60-80% humidity. The first 70 km before Gibraltar Range are energy-sapping in sticky subtropical air. Second, the temperature differential: atop Gibraltar Range at 1,100 m, temperatures are 6-8C cooler and conditions are drier. If you have been sweating through the valley, the Range descent can chill you rapidly. The New England Tablelands from Glen Innes to Inverell are exposed to westerly winds and can be significantly cooler than the start. You are effectively riding through two climate zones in a single day. Rain is possible — March averages 90 mm around Grafton — and wet roads through the Range descents add genuine danger.

How adventurous is Squadron Energy Grafton to Inverell Cycle Classic?

+

A 228 km point-to-point journey through three distinct geographic zones is inherently adventurous. You start in the subtropical Clarence Valley at sea level, climb through World Heritage-listed Gondwana Rainforest on Gibraltar Range — ancient temperate rainforest that has existed since Australia was part of Gondwana — emerge onto the New England Tablelands at 1,100 m, traverse 100 km of open highland country through Glen Innes and across the granite belt, and descend into the sapphire-mining town of Inverell. The landscape transformation is dramatic and visible: from humid river flats to moss-draped rainforest to open grassland to rolling farmland. The Gwydir Highway is a journey, not just a route. You start in one world and finish in another.

Should I race Squadron Energy Grafton to Inverell Cycle Classic?

+

Yes if you are a competitive road cyclist who wants to test yourself against Australia's hardest one-day race — 228 km with serious climbing and genuine NRS-level racing. Yes if you want to ride a race with 65 years of unbroken history through some of the most dramatic terrain in New South Wales. Skip if you are not prepared for 228 km and 3,382 m of climbing. Skip if the remote logistics of regional NSW are a dealbreaker. Skip if you need a feed station every 30 km — self-sufficiency is part of the challenge.
