Facts & History

In 1981, the Wichita Falls Board of Commerce and Industry asked for ideas to celebrate the city's upcoming centennial. Roby Christie, a member of the newly formed Wichita Falls Bicycle Club, proposed a ride that would become a triple entendre: 100 miles, in 100-degree heat, to celebrate 100 years. Most century rides of that era drew 200-250 riders. Christie insisted on reaching 1,000. On August 27, 1982, 1,203 riders left the parking lot of Memorial Stadium in 98-degree heat, exceeding all expectations. The event has run every year since (virtual only in 2020 due to COVID-19), growing into the largest sanctioned century ride in the United States. Founded in 1982. By Roby Christie and the Wichita Falls Bicycle Club. Christie, a 37-year-old assistant postmaster and avid cyclist, proposed a century ride to celebrate Wichita Falls' 100th anniversary to the Board of Commerce and Industry in 1981..

Reputation: The original heat-challenge century ride. Four decades of continuous operation, the largest sanctioned century in America, and the birthplace of CamelBak have made HHH a permanent fixture in American cycling culture. Earning a Texas Historical Marker and USA Today's Best Road Cycling Event recognition cemented its status as more than a ride — it is an institution.

1982: Inaugural ride — 1,203 riders start from Memorial Stadium in 98-degree heat, far exceeding the 1,000-rider goal
1988: Paramedic Michael Eidson invents the prototype for CamelBak at HHH — an IV bag in a tube sock stuffed in his jersey with a hose clamped by a clothespin
2009: Peak participation — over 14,000 riders registered for the weekend events
2020: First virtual-only year due to COVID-19 pandemic — 2,500 riders participated remotely
2021: Return to in-person riding with virtual option maintained
2024: Voted 'Best Road Cycling Event' by USA Today readers in the Reader's Choice Awards
2025: 44th edition — over 8,000 participants; Les Akins (age 61) posts fastest century time of 4:04:49

The Course

One hundred miles of flat, exposed North Texas prairie in conditions that redefine the word 'hot.' The course loops through Wichita and Clay counties on paved farm-to-market roads, starting and finishing in downtown Wichita Falls. There are no climbs worth naming, no technical features, no scenic distractions — just relentless sun, wind, and heat radiating off pavement. The challenge is entirely atmospheric. The route's most famous feature is Hell's Gate at mile 61, where riders must arrive by 12:30 PM or be diverted to the shorter 75-mile route. Everything about this course is designed to test whether you can manage your body temperature, hydration, and pacing across 100 miles of hostile conditions.

Signature challenge: The heat. Full stop. One hundred miles on flat roads is a standard century. One hundred miles on flat roads at 100-108F with a heat index over 111F and relentless wind is a survival exercise that tests hydration strategy, heat acclimatization, and mental resilience in ways that climbing and technical terrain cannot. CamelBak was invented here because the conditions demanded a solution that did not yet exist. That tells you everything.

MILE
Mass Start — Downtown Wichita Falls
Seven-oh-five AM. Ten thousand riders roll out on Scott Avenue in the relative cool of early morning (80-85F). The energy is electric, the pace too fast, and the temptation to overcook the first 20 miles is real. Experienced riders settle in and save matches — the heat has not arrived yet, but it is coming.
30
MILE
The Heat Arrives
By mile 30, the temperature has been climbing steadily and crosses 95F. The shade is gone — there was never much to begin with. The wind picks up from the southwest, hot and gritty. Riders who skipped rest stops or underhydrated in the first 30 miles begin to feel it. The prairie stretches endlessly in every direction. This is where HHH begins to earn its name.
61
MILE
Hell's Gate
The most famous checkpoint in American century riding. Arrive by 12:30 PM or you are diverted to the 75-mile route. By now the temperature has passed 100F and may be pushing 106-108F. Riders who make the cutoff face another 39 miles of increasing heat. Riders who miss it get a shorter but still brutal finish. The drama of Hell's Gate — the math of pacing, the anxiety of the clock, the relief or despair at the checkpoint — is the signature emotional moment of HHH.
75
MILE
The Furnace — Miles 70-90
The worst stretch. Peak afternoon heat (100-108F), accumulated fatigue, depleted hydration, and 15-25 more miles to go. The wind has not relented. The road shimmers with heat mirage. Aid stations every 10 miles are survival checkpoints, not convenience stops. Riders describe this stretch as 'the longest 20 miles of their lives.' Heat cramps, nausea, and mental fog are common. This is where HHH breaks people.
95
MILE
Final 5 — Return to Wichita Falls
The outskirts of Wichita Falls appear through the heat haze. Five miles to go. Riders who have survived the furnace dig deep for a finish that feels disproportionately emotional for a flat century ride. The MPEC finish line, the cheering crowds, the cold water, the air conditioning — all of it hits differently when you have been baking for 5-8 hours. You did not conquer a mountain. You survived a climate.

RACE-SPECIFIC: HOTTER'N HELL HUNDRED
HEAT PREP August in Wichita Falls, Texas, USA — The name says it all. Late August in Wichita Falls, Texas delivers average high temperatures of 97F with regular excursions past 100F and occasional spikes to 106-108F. The heat index — factoring in humidity — routinely exceeds 111F. Temperatures begin climbing past 100F as early as 9:45 AM, meaning 100-mile riders face 4-5 hours of triple-digit riding. August is the least humid month in Wichita Falls (average 50% relative humidity), but the dry heat is relentless and unshaded. Direct sun exposure on open prairie roads compounds the perceived temperature by 10-15F beyond the official heat index. This is North Texas — the wind never stops, but in August it blows hot, full of dust, and smells of baked asphalt and prairie. Begin heat acclimatization 10–14 days before Hotter'N Hell Hundred — 20–30min sauna sessions or midday rides in full kit. Pre-load sodium 48 hours out. Target 500–750ml/hr with electrolytes on race day. Race-day realities: Heat index exceeding 111F with direct sun exposure adding 10-15F; Dehydration and heat illness are serious medical risks — heat exhaustion and heatstroke send riders to medical tents every year.
NUTRITION Target 60–80g carbs/hour for Hotter'N Hell Hundred’s 100 miles. Start fueling within the first 30 minutes — early fueling prevents late-race collapse. Bonking at mile 60 is a nutrition failure, not a fitness failure.

Race Logistics

Airport
Wichita Falls Municipal Airport (SPS) — American Eagle commuter flights to DFW only, 6 flights daily. Most riders fly into Dallas/Fort Worth International Airport (DFW) and drive 2 hours north on I-44/US-287. Oklahoma City (OKC) is 3 hours northeast.
Lodging
Book early — Wichita Falls hotels sell out by March for HHH weekend. This is a near-100% occupancy event for the city. Call hotels directly using local numbers rather than national reservation systems or websites, which may show sold out when rooms exist. Delta Hotels by Marriott Wichita Falls Convention Center ($119+), Best Western North Side Inn ($68+), Fairfield Inn & Suites ($97+). Hotels within 60 miles of Wichita Falls are an option if the city proper is full. RV and camping accommodations also available.
Food
Texas does not let you go hungry. Wichita Falls has standard chain and local restaurants. Event rest stops provide water, electrolytes, fruit, and snacks at 19 locations. Pre-ride breakfast buffet at 5:00 AM on Saturday. Post-ride food and beverages at the finish festival. Hydration strategy is critical — plan to consume 1-2 bottles per hour minimum in the heat.
Packet Pickup
Wichita Falls Multi-Purpose Events Center (MPEC) and Convention Center. Packet pickup available Thursday through Saturday morning. Expo and trade show run concurrently.
Parking
Downtown Wichita Falls staging area. Specific parking instructions provided with registration. Arrive early on Saturday — 10,000+ riders converge on downtown.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at Hotter'N Hell Hundred?

+

This is the reason the event exists. The triple entendre in the name — 100 miles, 100 degrees, 100th anniversary — puts climate at the absolute center. Average August highs in Wichita Falls hit 97F. The heat index averages 111F. Temperatures on race day routinely pass 100F by 9:45 AM and can spike to 106-108F by late morning. Direct sun on open, unsheltered prairie roads adds 10-15F of perceived temperature. One rider described the wind as 'a hot hand in your face, full of dust and the ominous scent of road kills.' Heat exhaustion and heatstroke are annual medical realities. CamelBak was literally invented at this event because the heat was so extreme that a paramedic jury-rigged an IV bag into a hydration system. Climate is not a factor here — it is THE factor.

How are the logistics for Hotter'N Hell Hundred?

+

Forty-plus years of operational refinement shows. Nineteen staffed rest stops spaced every 10 miles keep riders hydrated and fed through the heat. The Hell's Gate cutoff at mile 61 is a smart safety mechanism that diverts slower riders before the worst afternoon heat. The multi-day festival is well-orchestrated — expo, criterium races, mountain bike races, gravel grind, trail runs, and the Saturday century all run smoothly for 12,000+ visitors. Registration is straightforward via BikeReg. The one knock: Wichita Falls is remote. The municipal airport (SPS) offers only American Eagle commuter flights to DFW. Most riders drive, and the 2-hour haul from DFW or 3 hours from OKC keeps this from a 5.

How adventurous is Hotter'N Hell Hundred?

+

Let us be honest: flat Texas farm roads through Wichita and Clay counties are not scenic adventure. The landscape is open prairie, agricultural land, scattered oil derricks, and small rural towns. There are no mountain vistas, no river crossings, no forest canopy. The adventure here is entirely internal — the battle against heat, wind, and your own body's desire to stop. The mass start with 10,000 riders is exhilarating, and the Hell's Gate drama at mile 61 adds genuine tension. But if adventure means scenic grandeur, this is not your event. If adventure means pushing yourself to a limit defined by nature's hostility, few events deliver more.

+

The largest sanctioned century ride in the United States. Running continuously since 1982 (except the 2020 COVID virtual pivot). Voted 'Best Road Cycling Event' by USA Today readers in 2024. Birthplace of CamelBak — a piece of cycling history invented on this very course in 1988. Has earned its own Texas Historical Marker. Generates $10 million in annual economic impact for Wichita Falls. The event has hosted professional criterium racing alongside the endurance ride for decades. Every serious century rider in America knows the name. It is not an international prestige event like Paris-Roubaix, but within American road cycling, 'I finished HHH in 100-degree heat' carries real weight.

Should I race Hotter'N Hell Hundred?

+

If you live in Texas or the southern plains, this is a mandatory bucket-list century. If you are flying in from out of state, it is worth the trip exactly once — to prove to yourself that you can ride 100 miles when the thermometer says you should not. Train your heat tolerance for at least 6 weeks beforehand. Arrive hydrated, start early, respect Hell's Gate at mile 61, and do not underestimate the Texas wind. The multi-day festival and Friday criterium make it a worthy cycling weekend even beyond the century ride.
