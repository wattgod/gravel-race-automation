The Ratings

Course Profile
Logistics 3/5

Idaho Springs sits 33 miles west of Denver on I-70 — one of the most accessible mountain towns in Colorado. Denver International Airport is a major hub with cheap flights from everywhere. The drive is easy. But Idaho Springs is a small town with limited lodging — a few motels, inns, and vacation rentals. Many riders stay in Denver or the I-70 corridor and drive up race morning. Packet pickup and staging are well-organized for a community event, but this is not a major metro start.

Editorial
Prestige 4/5

Running since 1962, this is one of the oldest continuously held cycling events in the United States. Named for Bob Cook, a five-time winner and US Olympian who died of brain cancer at 23, the memorial carries genuine emotional weight. Tom Danielson's 2004 course record (1:41:20) and Jeannie Longo's women's record stand as benchmarks. Within US hill climb circles, this is the event — the highest, the hardest, the most storied. It does not have European grand tour prestige, but within American cycling, completing Mt. Evans means something.

Facts & History

The Mt. Evans Hill Climb began in 1962 as a test of nerve and lungs on the highest paved road in North America. In 1981, the race was renamed in honor of Bob Cook, a five-time winner who held the course record from 1975 to 1980. Cook was a US Olympian — a prodigious talent who first set the record as a junior. He died of brain cancer at age 23. The memorial carries his name forward through every rider who makes the climb. In September 2023, the mountain was officially renamed Mount Blue Sky by the US Board on Geographic Names, honoring the Arapaho people (the 'Blue Sky People') and addressing the legacy of territorial governor John Evans and the Sand Creek Massacre. The race is now also known as the Blue Sky Hill Climb. Sixty-plus years of continuous running (with a handful of cancellations for snow, the 1996 Olympics, and 2025 road construction) makes this one of the most enduring cycling events in the United States. Founded in 1962. By Idaho Springs cycling community.

Reputation: The altitude race. The highest finish line on pavement in North American cycling. A memorial to a champion who ran out of time.

The Course

One direction: up. The Mt. Evans Hill Climb is 27.4 miles of continuous ascending from the town of Idaho Springs at 7,540 feet to the summit parking area at 14,130 feet. The gradient is never savage — averaging 4.5% with a maximum around 10% — but the relentlessness is the point. There are no descents, no flat recovery sections (save a brief respite at Echo Lake), and no escape from the altitude that builds with every pedal stroke. The course passes through three distinct life zones: montane forest, subalpine forest with ancient bristlecone pines, and finally barren alpine tundra where nothing grows and mountain goats outnumber cyclists. Above treeline at 11,500 feet, the landscape transforms into an exposed, wind-blasted moonscape. The final miles above 13,000 feet are ridden in a hypoxic fog where your body is operating on roughly 60% of sea-level oxygen.

Signature challenge: The final 5 miles above Summit Lake. From 12,830 feet to 14,130 feet, exposed switchbacks climb through alpine nothing. The oxygen is gone. The trees are gone. The gradient is honest (5.7% average) but your body has been robbed of its ability to process it. This is where the Mt. Evans Hill Climb separates itself from every other cycling event on the continent.

MILE
Idaho Springs — The Gun
Start at 7,540 feet in the mountain town of Idaho Springs. The climb begins immediately on CO-103. Spirits are high, legs are fresh, and the altitude feels irrelevant. It will not stay that way.
7
MILE
The Forest Wall
The grade steepens to 5.8% average through dense pine forest. The road switchbacks through shaded canyon walls. The air is thinning but you do not notice yet because the trees still feel normal. Your power meter begins to tell a different story than your legs.
13
MILE
Echo Lake — False Hope
A brief flat section at Echo Lake (10,600 ft) provides the only horizontal pedaling of the entire race. Catch your breath. Look at the alpine lake. Appreciate the trees — they are about to disappear. The real climb starts now.
18
MILE
Above Treeline — The Moonscape
At 11,500 feet, the trees end. Not thin out. End. The landscape transforms into exposed alpine tundra — rock, scrub, snowfields, and sky. Wind hits you for the first time without any windbreak. The grade averages 4-5% but your legs say 8%. Oxygen deficit is accumulating. Every pedal stroke costs more than the last.
22
MILE
Summit Lake — Oxygen Debt
Summit Lake sits at 12,830 feet. Mountain goats graze near the road, entirely indifferent to your suffering. The air has roughly 35% less oxygen than sea level. Your heart rate is elevated just sitting still. The final 5 miles and 1,300 feet of climbing loom above you on switchbacks carved into the mountainside. There is nowhere to hide.
27
MILE
The Summit — 14,130 Feet
The summit parking area at 14,130 feet. You are 130 feet below the true peak of Mt. Blue Sky. The air pressure is roughly 60% of sea level. Some riders cannot unclip. Some cannot speak. Some cry. You have ridden your bicycle higher than any paved road in North America will take you. The Continental Divide stretches in every direction. It is cold. It is windy. It is the most altitude you will ever reach on a bike.

The road is the highest paved road in the United States reaching about 14,500 feet elevation.

Race Logistics

Airport
Denver International Airport (DEN) — 65 miles, ~75 minutes via I-70. One of the largest US hubs with cheap domestic and international flights.
Lodging
Idaho Springs has limited lodging — motels and inns from $86-150/night. Book early. Many riders stay in Denver or the I-70 corridor (Georgetown, Empire, Evergreen) and drive up race morning. The 33-mile drive is straightforward on I-70 westbound.
Food
Idaho Springs has several restaurants and the historic mining-town downtown. Post-race options available. Bring cold-weather nutrition — gels and bars that work at altitude and in cold.
Packet Pickup
Pre-race day in Idaho Springs. USA Cycling license verification required.
Parking
Idaho Springs start area. Arrive early — limited parking for ~500 riders.
RIDERS REPORT
Expect about five hours to reach the top and one hour for the descent.
It's cold at the top so prepare for temperature changes during the climb.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at Bob Cook Memorial Mt. Evans Hill Climb (Blue Sky Hill Climb)?

+

The climate challenge here is unlike any other cycling event. You start in comfortable summer warmth at 7,540 ft and finish in conditions that can feel like winter at 14,130 ft. Temperature drops of 30-40 degrees Fahrenheit are standard. Above treeline, wind exposure is total — there are no trees, no shelter, nothing between you and whatever the mountain decides to throw at you. Snow, sleet, and hail in July are not unusual. Hypothermia risk is real at the summit. You need arm warmers, a vest, and gloves for a July bike race.

How are the logistics for Bob Cook Memorial Mt. Evans Hill Climb (Blue Sky Hill Climb)?

+

Idaho Springs sits 33 miles west of Denver on I-70 — one of the most accessible mountain towns in Colorado. Denver International Airport is a major hub with cheap flights from everywhere. The drive is easy. But Idaho Springs is a small town with limited lodging — a few motels, inns, and vacation rentals. Many riders stay in Denver or the I-70 corridor and drive up race morning. Packet pickup and staging are well-organized for a community event, but this is not a major metro start.

How adventurous is Bob Cook Memorial Mt. Evans Hill Climb (Blue Sky Hill Climb)?

+

You ride a bicycle to the summit of a fourteener. You pass through three distinct life zones — from pine forest to ancient bristlecone pines to barren alpine tundra where nothing grows. Mountain goats wander onto the road near the summit and do not care about your Strava time. The landscape above treeline is lunar — exposed rock, snowfields in July, and nothing but sky above you. The final miles are among the most otherworldly terrain accessible on a bicycle anywhere on Earth.

How prestigious is Bob Cook Memorial Mt. Evans Hill Climb (Blue Sky Hill Climb)?

+

Running since 1962, this is one of the oldest continuously held cycling events in the United States. Named for Bob Cook, a five-time winner and US Olympian who died of brain cancer at 23, the memorial carries genuine emotional weight. Tom Danielson's 2004 course record (1:41:20) and Jeannie Longo's women's record stand as benchmarks. Within US hill climb circles, this is the event — the highest, the hardest, the most storied. It does not have European grand tour prestige, but within American cycling, completing Mt. Evans means something.

Should I race Bob Cook Memorial Mt. Evans Hill Climb (Blue Sky Hill Climb)?

+

Yes if you want the most extreme altitude experience available in competitive cycling on paved roads. Yes if you have trained for altitude or can arrive early enough to acclimatize. Skip if you have altitude sickness history, need technical terrain variety, or want a mass-participation atmosphere.
