The Ratings

Course Profile
Logistics 3/5

Enumclaw is a small foothill town about 60 minutes southeast of Seattle-Tacoma International Airport (SEA). SeaTac is a major hub with abundant domestic and international connections — getting to Seattle is easy. Getting to Enumclaw is the friction. It is a one-highway town at the base of the Cascades with limited hotel inventory. The Rodeway Inn, GuestHouse, and a handful of small motels are your options — book months ahead or they are gone. Auburn and Puyallup (30 minutes west) offer more chain hotels but lose the pre-ride convenience. The start at the Enumclaw Expo Center is well-organized after 40+ years of practice. Packet pickup the day before. Parking is straightforward at the fairgrounds. The loop course means your car is where you left it — a massive logistical advantage over point-to-point events. The biggest complexity is the NPS permitting situation, which has produced 13 different route editions and multiple cancellations. You may register for one route and ride another, or not ride at all.

Editorial
Prestige 4/5

RAMROD has been the Pacific Northwest's defining cycling challenge since John Dixon organized the first ride in 1984 with 52 starters. Forty-one editions later, it sells out through a lottery system with an 800-rider NPS permit cap. Saying you have done RAMROD carries weight in PNW cycling circles the way saying you have done L'Etape or the Haute Route carries weight in Europe. The lottery creates scarcity — not everyone who wants to ride can ride. Volunteering to guarantee a future entry is a commitment that demonstrates dedication. The event was a timed race for its first decade, giving it competitive roots that most charity rides lack. The ongoing NPS permitting battle — with 2024 moved to September, 2025 rerouted, and 2026 canceled entirely — has, paradoxically, increased its prestige. The event is not internationally famous, which keeps it at 4 rather than 5.

Facts & History

In 1984, cyclist John Dixon organized a ride around Mount Rainier with 52 starters. Forty-five of them finished. The original course began and ended at the rustic fairgrounds in Enumclaw and was used without change for the first seven years. For its first decade, RAMROD was a timed race — an actual competition with results and rankings. In 1994, the event transitioned to an untimed endurance ride, reflecting a shift toward accessibility and the shared experience of the circumnavigation over competitive results. The Redmond Cycling Club has organized the event since its inception, building one of the most devoted volunteer cycling communities in the Pacific Northwest. The National Park Service's special use permit has always capped the field at 800 riders, creating a lottery system that generates scarcity and devotion in equal measure. Over 41 editions, 13 different routes have been used — clockwise, counterclockwise, with and without Paradise, with various bypass configurations — all dictated by the ongoing negotiation between a volunteer cycling club and the federal government over access to roads inside one of America's most visited national parks. That negotiation has become existential: after the NPS denied the 2024 permit, the cycling community rallied. The 2024 edition was moved to September. The 2025 edition was rerouted clockwise without the Paradise corridor. The 2026 edition has been canceled entirely. The Redmond Cycling Club calls it a pause, not an ending, and is fighting for a 2027 return that includes Stevens Canyon and a reasonable date. Founded in 1984. By John Dixon and the Redmond Cycling Club.

Reputation: The Pacific Northwest's most iconic cycling challenge. Forty years of circumnavigating an active volcano. A lottery-entry event that sells out instantly and inspires lifelong devotion. Currently fighting for its survival against federal park bureaucracy.

The Course

RAMROD is a circumnavigation. You ride all the way around an active volcano and come back to where you started. That simple premise — one loop, one mountain, one day — produces one of the most varied and rewarding days you will ever spend on a bicycle. The ride begins in the agricultural lowlands east of Tacoma, where Enumclaw sits at the Cascade foothills. Depending on the route edition, you may head counterclockwise toward the Nisqually Entrance and Paradise (the classic route) or clockwise toward Crystal Mountain and Cayuse Pass (the modern bypass). Either way, the terrain transitions dramatically: flat farmland gives way to rolling foothills, which give way to towering old-growth forest, which gives way to alpine passes where the air cools and the mountain dominates the sky. Stevens Canyon Road — when permitted — is the emotional heart of the ride: a 1930s-era park road through a glacial valley with stone bridges and tunnels. Cayuse Pass at mile 100+ is the physical heart: 8 miles at 6% when your legs have nothing left. The long descent back to Enumclaw in the Pacific Northwest's extended summer twilight is the reward. The mountain was always there. You rode all the way around it.

Signature challenge: Cayuse Pass at mile 100. Eight miles at a constant 6% grade in afternoon sun with no shade, on legs that have already climbed 6,000-7,000 feet over 100 miles. Every RAMROD veteran knows the name. The rest stop halfway up Cayuse is the most grateful you will ever be for a cup of water and a folding chair. The climb is not technically difficult — it is 6% and relentless — but arriving at its base after 100 miles of riding around a volcano transforms a moderate climb into a test of will. Cayuse Pass is where RAMROD separates the people who trained for 150 miles from the people who trained for 100.

MILE
Enumclaw — 5 AM in the Dark
The Enumclaw Expo Center parking lot at 4 AM. Eight hundred people eating Rotary club breakfast under fluorescent lights. The air is 50 degrees and the mountain is invisible in the pre-dawn darkness. At 5 AM, you roll out into the foothills. The first 20-30 miles are flat to rolling farmland and state highway — legs feel incredible, the group energy is high, and you are thinking about the 130 miles still to come. Discipline here is everything: the temptation to ride 18 mph in a group will cost you 10 hours later.
30
MILE
The Foothills — Into the Trees
The terrain transitions from open farmland to forested foothills. Depending on the route, you are either climbing toward the Nisqually Entrance or heading southeast toward Crystal Mountain. The roads narrow. The trees grow taller. Douglas fir and western red cedar close in overhead. The first significant climbing begins — nothing savage, but the gradual vertical accumulation that will add up to 10,000 feet by day's end starts here. You are entering Mount Rainier's ecosystem.
55
MILE
Paradise Climb (Classic Route) — Cathedral Forest
On the traditional route, the climb from the Nisqually Entrance to Paradise is 18 miles of sustained ascending through old-growth forest — some of the largest trees in the Pacific Northwest. The road switchbacks through ancient Douglas fir and western hemlock, gaining over 3,000 feet. The gradient is steady (4-5% average) but relentless. Above treeline, Mount Rainier appears — all 14,411 feet of glaciated volcanic cone, close enough to make you feel insignificant. The Paradise rest stop at 5,400 feet is the highest point on the route and one of the most spectacular aid stations in American cycling.
75
MILE
Stevens Canyon — The Crown Jewel
The descent from Paradise into Stevens Canyon is the emotional peak of RAMROD. The 1930s-era park road features stone bridges, tunnels, and viaducts built in the National Park Service rustic style. The glacial valley unfolds below — immense, ancient, and quiet. The road drops through old-growth forest along a glacial river. Box Canyon rest stop provides water and relief. This is the section that the Redmond Cycling Club has been fighting to preserve in the NPS permitting battle — without Stevens Canyon, RAMROD loses its soul.
100
MILE
Cayuse Pass — The Breaking Point
The name that lives in infamy for every RAMROD veteran. You arrive at the base of Cayuse Pass at approximately mile 100, at the hottest part of the day, with the sun on your back, having already climbed 6,000-7,000 feet. The climb is 8 miles at a constant 6% grade, rising from 2,400 to 4,675 feet, with almost no shade. There is a rest stop halfway up — it is the most appreciated rest stop on the course. Your glycogen stores are depleted. Your legs are filing formal complaints. The mountain does not care. You grind up Cayuse Pass because there is no other way to get around the volcano.
110
MILE
Chinook Pass — The Final Summit
From Cayuse Pass, the traditional route continues to Chinook Pass at 5,432 feet — the highest point on the course. SR 410 skirts Tipsoo Lake with panoramic Cascade Range views. At this elevation, the air is noticeably thinner than the valley floor. You have been riding for 8-12 hours. The climb to Chinook is shorter and less brutal than Cayuse, but it arrives on legs that have already been destroyed. The summit marks the beginning of the end: the long descent back toward Enumclaw begins here.
130
MILE
The Descent — Earned Twilight
From Chinook Pass, SR 410 descends steadily toward the Puget Sound lowlands. The road drops over 3,000 feet in approximately 25 miles. Your exhausted legs spin freely for the first time in hours. The trees change from subalpine fir back to Douglas fir. The air warms. Enumclaw materializes in the late-afternoon light. The final 10 miles of flat valley road back to the Expo Center feel like either a victory lap or a death march, depending on how well you managed the previous 140 miles. Ice cream is waiting.

Race Logistics

Airport
Seattle-Tacoma International Airport (SEA) — 60 minutes north. Major domestic and international hub with abundant flight options. Portland International (PDX) — 3.5 hours south, viable as a backup.
Lodging
Enumclaw has very limited hotel inventory — the Rodeway Inn, GuestHouse Enumclaw, and a few small motels are your options. Book the moment you win the lottery or they will be gone. Overflow strategy: Auburn (30 min west) and Puyallup (35 min west) have chain hotels with more availability. Some riders stay in Tacoma or Federal Way and drive in for the 5 AM start. The Enumclaw Expo Center parking lot fills early — arrive by 4:00 AM.
Food
Enumclaw is a small town with basic dining: pizza, burgers, a few local restaurants. Carb-loading options are limited compared to a major city. The pre-ride breakfast at the start — hard-boiled eggs, hash browns, sausage, oatmeal, coffee, OJ — is provided by the Enumclaw Rotary and Lions clubs and is a beloved tradition. On-course aid stations provide food and water at multiple points. Ice cream at the finish line. Bring your own nutrition for 150+ miles — the aid stations supplement, they do not replace your fueling strategy.
Packet Pickup
Day before the ride at the Enumclaw Expo Center. In-person only. Includes route maps, rider packet, and event information for the specific route edition being used that year.
Parking
Enumclaw Expo Center (formerly Enumclaw Fairgrounds). Arrive by 4:00 AM for the 5:00 AM start. Loop course — your car will be there when you return. Free parking.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at RAMROD — Ride Around Mount Rainier in One Day?

+

Late July in the Mount Rainier corridor is the best weather the Pacific Northwest offers — which means mostly dry, warm days with highs of 75-85F in the valleys and 55-65F at the passes. The early 5 AM start in Enumclaw is cool (50-55F) and requires layers. The exposed Cayuse Pass climb in the afternoon sun can be genuinely hot, particularly with no shade for miles. Mount Rainier is a 14,411-foot volcano that generates its own weather: clouds, fog, and rain showers can appear at any elevation without warning, even on a forecast-clear day. The temperature swing from valley floor to alpine pass summit demands planning — you will need different clothing for different parts of the ride. In recent years, late July wildfire smoke from Eastern Washington and Oregon has occasionally settled over the Cascade foothills, degrading air quality. The climate is manageable but demands respect.

How adventurous is RAMROD — Ride Around Mount Rainier in One Day?

+

You are riding your bicycle around Mount Rainier. An active volcano. The most glaciated peak in the contiguous United States. A mountain so massive it creates its own weather systems. The ride takes you through old-growth forests where trees are 500 years old, past glacial rivers running milky with volcanic sediment, over alpine passes where the air thins and the Cascade Range unfolds in every direction, and through Stevens Canyon — a glacial valley with 1930s stone bridges and tunnels that look like they were built for a different century. You pass through tiny PNW foothills towns — Eatonville, Elbe, Ashford — where the local economy exists because Rainier exists. The mountain is always there: looming above the treeline, dominating the horizon, reminding you that you are a very small person on a very small bicycle riding around something ancient and indifferent. This is one of the great adventure rides in the United States.

Should I race RAMROD — Ride Around Mount Rainier in One Day?

+

Yes if you want to circumnavigate an active volcano in a single day on one of America's most storied cycling routes. Yes if 150+ miles through national park alpine passes, old-growth forest, and glacial valleys is your idea of a perfect day. Yes if you value community tradition over timing chips and age-group podiums. Enter the lottery every year and volunteer when you can. Skip if you need competitive structure, technical terrain, or a ride shorter than 150 miles. Skip if you cannot handle uncertainty — the route changes annually and the event's future depends on National Park Service permitting decisions.
