Facts & History

The Seattle to Portland Bicycle Classic was born in 1979, organized by the Cascade Bicycle Club as a competitive time-trial race from Seattle City Hall to Portland City Hall. The inaugural event attracted roughly 100 starters, with 69 finishers. Jerry Baker won the first edition, completing the 206-mile course in just over 10 hours. By 1981, Cascade Bicycle Club converted STP from a race to a recreational ride, opening it to cyclists of all abilities. The decision transformed STP from a niche competition into a mass-participation event. Participation peaked at 10,000 riders in 1989 — the event's tenth year — and STP has remained one of the largest recreational cycling events in America ever since. The ride has run every year since 1979 except 2020 and 2021, when COVID-19 forced cancellations. Even the eruption of Mount St. Helens in 1980 only rerouted the course — it didn't stop the ride. Founded in 1979. By Cascade Bicycle Club.

Reputation: The Pacific Northwest's cycling pilgrimage. One of America's ten largest recreational rides. The event that turned 206 miles of country road into a regional tradition.

The Course

A 206-mile south-bound corridor from Seattle to Portland on paved country roads, avoiding Interstate 5. The route threads through the green lowlands of western Washington and Oregon — Puget Sound farmland, the Nisqually and Chehalis river valleys, the Cowlitz corridor, and the Columbia River crossing into Oregon. The terrain is predominantly flat to gently rolling, with one honest climb near Puyallup and energy-sapping rollers between Chehalis and Napavine. Small towns punctuate the route every 15-25 miles, each one a checkpoint and a morale boost. The course crosses from Washington into Oregon via the Lewis and Clark Bridge over the Columbia River. The final miles run through St. Helens and Scappoose before crossing Portland's St. Johns Bridge to the finish at Holladay Park. The character is rural, green, and relentlessly forward — 206 miles in one direction, no turning back.

Signature challenge: The distance itself. STP has no single feature that defines the course — no brutal climb, no technical descent, no unrideable sector. The challenge is 206 miles of sustained forward motion, managing nutrition and hydration across 8-14 hours of saddle time, and the psychological discipline of riding one direction for an entire day (or two). The Chehalis-to-Napavine rollers on tired legs are the closest thing to a crux, but the real opponent is the odometer.

MILE
The Mass Start — University of Washington
Rolling out from UW's parking lot E-18 with thousands of riders in the cool Seattle morning. The first 10 miles are the most crowded — finding your pace group, navigating the urban exit from Seattle, and settling in for a very long day (or two). Seward Park at mile 10 is the first mini-stop. The adrenaline of the mass start masks the reality of what's ahead: 196 more miles.
42
MILE
The Big Hill — Puyallup
The only real climb on the entire route. A mile-long grind at roughly 7% grade near Puyallup — not steep by any standard except the standard of a ride that is otherwise dead flat. First-timers are shocked. Veterans barely notice. The first major food stop at Spanaway Middle School is nearby. This is the last significant vertical you'll see for 160 miles.
88
MILE
The Approach to Centralia — Tenino to the Midpoint
Tenino City Park at mile 88, then the psychological milestone: Centralia College at mile 101, the official midpoint. For two-day riders, this is the finish line — camping, showers, food vendors, beer garden, and the strange communal experience of sleeping in a field with thousands of other cyclists. For one-day riders, this is where the ride gets real. You're exactly halfway. The second hundred miles starts now.
108
MILE
The Rolling Grind — Chehalis to Napavine
The most fatiguing section of the route. Deceptive rolling terrain between Chehalis and Napavine that drains tired legs. None of the rollers are individually hard, but the cumulative effect on mile-108 legs is significant. This is where riders who started too fast begin to crack. The flat sections between rollers offer false hope. Keep eating. Keep drinking. Keep pedaling.
163
MILE
The Columbia Corridor — Longview to Goble
Crossing the Columbia River from Washington into Oregon marks a psychological shift — you're in Portland's state now. The Lewis and Clark Bridge is a moment. The route follows the Columbia River corridor through Goble, where the terrain flattens out and the miles begin counting down toward Portland. Legs are heavy, the day is long, but the finish is within reach.
188
MILE
The Final Push — Scappoose to Portland
The last 18 miles from Scappoose into Portland. The route runs through St. Helens and along Highway 30 toward the city. The final climb to cross the St. Johns Bridge is a cruel joke after 200 miles — not long, not steep, but your legs will disagree. Then you're on the bridge, looking down at the Willamette River, and Portland is right there. The descent to Holladay Park. The finish arch. The medal. The beer. 206 miles, done.
RIDERS REPORT
Puyallup Hill
The main hill on the ride at about 300 feet elevation gain with a 5.7% gradient. Riders describe it as their chance for glory and the tallest climb on the course.
JBLM Area and Yelm Trail
Features the most stop and go riding with lots of stop signs, bollards, dividers, yellow squares on the ground, bumps, and roots like any other trail. You can't go as fast through this section.
Columbia River Bridge
One rider called crossing this bridge the scariest thing they've ever done on a bicycle, with uncertainty about whether they could cross without being in traffic lanes.
Roads were wet and drizzling during the ride, creating slippery conditions. The course is mostly paved roads with a few gravel sections scattered throughout.
The Yelm trail section has bollards, dividers, yellow squares on the ground, bumps, and roots just like any other trail, preventing riders from maintaining higher speeds.

Race Logistics

Airport
Seattle-Tacoma International Airport (SEA) — one of the West Coast's busiest airports with direct flights from virtually every major US city and many international destinations. Light rail connects SEA to the University of Washington start area. Portland International Airport (PDX) is convenient for the return trip, with Amtrak Cascades or bus service back to Seattle.
Lodging
You need lodging in Seattle the night before the start — University District hotels are closest to the UW start, or stay downtown and take the light rail. Two-day riders camp overnight at Centralia College (included with registration — camping area, food vendors, showers, beer garden). Book a Portland hotel for Saturday night if riding two days. One-day riders need Portland lodging for Saturday night. Both options require planning your return to Seattle — Amtrak Cascades, bus, or arrange a ride. Budget $150-250/night in Seattle or Portland.
Food
Rest stops provide free food approximately every 25 miles. Five major food stations along the route. The Centralia overnight camp has food vendors and a beer garden. The Portland finish at Holladay Park includes a meal, beer garden, and celebration. Seattle and Portland both have exceptional dining — PNW cuisine, craft beer, and coffee culture. Pre-ride carb loading options are endless in both cities.
Packet Pickup
Event weekend at the University of Washington campus, Seattle. Check cascade.org for specific times and location details.
Parking
UW campus parking available (sold as add-on). Arrive early — 6,000+ riders competing for spots. Light rail from SEA-TAC is a parking-free alternative. Remember: your car will be in Seattle and you'll finish in Portland. Plan accordingly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at STP — Seattle to Portland?

+

Mid-July in the PNW is usually excellent cycling weather — warm, dry, long daylight hours. But the heat waves that have hit the Pacific Northwest in recent summers have changed the calculus. STP 2023 saw 92F both days, with widespread heat exhaustion and high dropout rates. When the weather cooperates, it's a 75F dream. When it doesn't, 206 miles of exposed rural roads in 90F+ heat is a serious survival exercise. The southerly headwind that commonly blows from Portland toward Seattle means you're often riding into the wind — a persistent energy tax that the flat terrain makes worse.

How are the logistics for STP — Seattle to Portland?

+

Seattle-Tacoma International Airport (SEA) is one of the best-connected airports on the West Coast — direct flights from virtually everywhere. The start at the University of Washington is accessible via light rail from the airport. Portland (PDX) is equally well-connected for the return. Cascade Bicycle Club has been running this event since 1979 — 47 years of logistics refinement. Packet pickup, start logistics, rest stops, overnight camping at Centralia, baggage trucks, and finish-line operations are all dialed. Transportation add-ons (parking, bus passes, bike transport back to Seattle) are available. The only friction is the one-way logistics — you finish in Portland, 180 miles from your car in Seattle. Plan your return.

How adventurous is STP — Seattle to Portland?

+

The PNW countryside is pleasant but not spectacular on this route. You're riding through the Puget Sound lowlands, the Chehalis River valley, the Cowlitz River corridor, and the Willamette Valley — green farmland, small towns, Douglas fir forests, and river crossings. It's a pretty ride, not a jaw-dropping one. The adventure is in the journey itself: 206 miles city-to-city, passing through a dozen small towns, sleeping in a field in Centralia, and rolling into Portland under your own power. The finish across the St. Johns Bridge has a cinematic quality.

How prestigious is STP — Seattle to Portland?

+

STP has been running since 1979 and has drawn up to 10,000 riders in peak years. Bicycling Magazine has called it 'one of the best cycling events in the nation.' In the Pacific Northwest, 'I rode STP' carries real weight — it's a rite of passage for PNW cyclists, a line on the bucket list. Nationally, it's recognized as one of the ten largest recreational rides in America. But it's a participatory ride, not a race — the prestige is in the tradition and the accomplishment, not in a competitive result.

Should I race STP — Seattle to Portland?

+

Yes if you want one of America's great city-to-city cycling experiences. Yes if community and tradition matter more than competition. Yes if riding 206 miles through the PNW countryside with thousands of fellow cyclists sounds like your idea of a perfect July weekend. Skip if you need racing, climbing, technical terrain, or anything beyond flat-road endurance.
