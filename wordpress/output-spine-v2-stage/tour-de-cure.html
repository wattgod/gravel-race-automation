The Ratings

Course Profile
Logistics 4/5

Over 30 years of event production across dozens of editions has created a reliable, well-oiled operation. Each Tour de Cure edition is fully supported with rest stops every 10-20 miles, SAG vehicles, bike mechanics, volunteer route marshals, medical support, and a substantial post-ride festival. The American Diabetes Association's organizational infrastructure — corporate sponsors, regional teams, local volunteer networks — provides a level of support that most single-day charity rides cannot match. Multiple distance options at every edition accommodate everyone from families on a 5-mile fun ride to century riders. The nationwide franchise model means quality is consistent even as local flavor varies. Not quite at the PMC or AIDS/LifeCycle level of logistical complexity (those events move thousands of people over multiple days), but for a single-day charity ride, the Tour de Cure is exceptionally well-organized.

Editorial
Prestige 3/5

Tour de Cure is the American Diabetes Association's premier cycling event and one of the largest charity cycling series in the United States. At its peak in 2011, 55,000 cyclists participated in 80 events raising $18 million in a single year. The ADA brand carries institutional weight — diabetes affects 37 million Americans, and the Tour de Cure is how the cycling community responds. It does not carry the singular iconic status of the Pan-Mass Challenge ($1B+ raised) or the cultural gravity of AIDS/LifeCycle, but within the charity cycling landscape, Tour de Cure is a recognized and respected name. Completing a century at Tour de Cure earns genuine respect.

Facts & History

The American Diabetes Association launched Tour de Cure in 1991 as its premier cycling fundraising event, bringing the cycling community together to raise money and awareness for diabetes research, prevention, and education. The concept was simple and powerful: ride your bike, raise money, fight diabetes. From a handful of regional events, Tour de Cure grew into a nationwide series held across 44 states at its peak. In 2007, the Red Rider program was created by Mari Ruddy, a rider in the Colorado Tour de Cure, to recognize and celebrate participants living with type 1 or type 2 diabetes — transforming them from riders into symbols of courage and resilience. By 2011, the event reached its peak with more than 55,000 cyclists participating in 80 events, raising over $18 million in a single year. Over three decades, Tour de Cure has cumulatively raised hundreds of millions of dollars for the American Diabetes Association's mission. The event series has consolidated in recent years, with fewer but more focused regional editions, but the core mission remains unchanged: get on your bike and ride to stop diabetes. Founded in 1991. By American Diabetes Association.

The Course

Tour de Cure courses are designed for mass participation, not athletic challenge. Every edition offers multiple distance options from 5 to 100 miles, all on paved roads with gentle-to-flat terrain. The Arizona edition is representative: starting from Goodyear Ballpark, riders head into the Estrella foothills on smooth desert roads with long sightlines and minimal traffic, then take a unique lap around the Phoenix Raceway NASCAR track before returning to the ballpark for the festival. The Northern California edition winds through the oak-dotted hills and quiet roads of Woodside and Portola Valley — some of the most scenic cycling in the Bay Area. Chicagoland routes follow the Fox River Valley through Illinois farmland. New England courses traverse Massachusetts countryside from Lexington. The terrain is universally forgiving — these are rides designed so that a first-time cyclist on the 5-mile family route and a century rider on the 100-mile route can both finish with smiles, finisher medals, and the knowledge that their effort matters.

Signature challenge: Tour de Cure's signature challenge is not a climb or a headwind — it is the decision to ride. To register, raise $250, show up, and pedal alongside people who live with diabetes every day. The physical effort is modest by design. The emotional weight of riding for 37 million Americans with diabetes, riding alongside Red Riders who are fighting the disease while they ride — that is the challenge that matters.

MILE
The Start — Roll Out
Every Tour de Cure begins with a mass start from the host venue — Goodyear Ballpark in Arizona, HP campus in Palo Alto, Pottawatomie Park in St. Charles. The energy is festive: Red Riders in distinctive jerseys, corporate teams in matching kits, families with kids on tag-alongs. The first miles are flat and social as the field spreads out across the opening roads.
25
MILE
The Mid-Route — Finding Your Rhythm
By mile 25, the shorter-distance riders have peeled off to their turnarounds and the metric century and century riders settle into their pace. Rest stops appear every 10-20 miles with water, fruit, and encouragement. The terrain rolls gently. This is the meditative stretch — the miles where you remember why you are riding.
50
MILE
The Long Route — Earning It
Only the century and metric century riders see mile 50. The terrain may include the most significant rollers of the day — the Estrella foothills in Arizona, the Portola Valley climbs in Northern California, the rolling farmland of the Fox Valley in Illinois. Nothing brutal, but at 50 miles on a warm day, even gentle terrain earns respect. The rest stops become more welcome.
62
MILE
The Finish — Festival and Celebration
The finish line is a party. Cheering volunteers, finisher medals, catered lunch, beer garden, live music, health and wellness village, farmers market, kids zone. Red Riders are celebrated at dedicated tents. Corporate teams gather for photos. The post-ride festival transforms the finish from an ending into a beginning — the moment your miles become dollars become research become hope.

Race Logistics

Airport
Varies by edition. Arizona: Phoenix Sky Harbor (PHX), 25 miles to Goodyear Ballpark. Northern California: San Francisco (SFO) or San Jose (SJC), both within 30 miles of Palo Alto. Chicagoland: Chicago O'Hare (ORD) or Midway (MDW), ~45 miles to St. Charles. New England: Boston Logan (BOS), 15 miles to Lexington. Most participants drive to their local edition.
Lodging
Single-day events — no overnight required. Most participants drive from home to the start venue. For out-of-town riders, book a hotel near the start location the night before. Arizona: Goodyear/Litchfield Park hotels. Northern California: Palo Alto/Mountain View hotels. Chicagoland: St. Charles/Geneva hotels. New England: Lexington/Burlington hotels.
Food
Rest stops every 10-20 miles with water, electrolyte drinks, fruit, and snacks. Post-ride festival includes a complimentary catered lunch. Beer garden for adults. Vendors and food trucks at many editions. You will not go hungry.
Packet Pickup
Typically available on event morning at the start venue. Some editions offer pre-event pickup. Check your specific edition's page on tour.diabetes.org for details.
Parking
Free parking at all start venues. Arrive early for best spots — parking fills quickly at larger editions. Carpooling encouraged.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate like at Tour de Cure?

+

The nationwide format means some editions face climate challenges and others are weather-perfect. Arizona in February is about as ideal as cycling weather gets — dry, mild, sunny. But Chicagoland in August means Midwest humidity and potential heat advisories. New England in August is similarly hot and humid. Texas in October can still deliver real heat. No single edition faces extreme conditions, and the organizers select dates and locations to avoid the worst weather windows. But sun exposure on open roads is universal across all editions, and hydration discipline matters for the longer distances.

How are the logistics for Tour de Cure?

+

Over 30 years of event production across dozens of editions has created a reliable, well-oiled operation. Each Tour de Cure edition is fully supported with rest stops every 10-20 miles, SAG vehicles, bike mechanics, volunteer route marshals, medical support, and a substantial post-ride festival. The American Diabetes Association's organizational infrastructure — corporate sponsors, regional teams, local volunteer networks — provides a level of support that most single-day charity rides cannot match. Multiple distance options at every edition accommodate everyone from families on a 5-mile fun ride to century riders. The nationwide franchise model means quality is consistent even as local flavor varies. Not quite at the PMC or AIDS/LifeCycle level of logistical complexity (those events move thousands of people over multiple days), but for a single-day charity ride, the Tour de Cure is exceptionally well-organized.

How adventurous is Tour de Cure?

+

Tour de Cure routes pass through pleasant scenery — Arizona's Sonoran Desert foothills, Silicon Valley's oak-dotted hills, Illinois farmland, Massachusetts countryside. The NASCAR track lap in Arizona adds a unique novelty. But these are suburban and exurban roads, not backcountry wilderness. The adventure is social and emotional — riding alongside Red Riders who live with diabetes every day, raising money for a cause that affects 37 million Americans, and crossing a finish line knowing your effort matters. The geographic adventure is modest; the human adventure is real.

How prestigious is Tour de Cure?

+

Tour de Cure is the American Diabetes Association's premier cycling event and one of the largest charity cycling series in the United States. At its peak in 2011, 55,000 cyclists participated in 80 events raising $18 million in a single year. It does not carry the singular iconic status of the Pan-Mass Challenge ($1B+ raised) or the cultural gravity of AIDS/LifeCycle, but within the charity cycling landscape, Tour de Cure is a recognized and respected name. Completing a century at Tour de Cure earns genuine respect.

Should I race Tour de Cure?

+

You do not race Tour de Cure — you ride it with purpose. Yes if you want a well-organized, fully supported charity ride with a low barrier to entry ($250 fundraising minimum) and routes from 5 to 100 miles. Yes if you want to ride alongside Red Riders who live with diabetes and channel your cycling into something meaningful. Yes if you want a post-ride festival with food, music, and community. Skip if you need challenging terrain, competitive timing, or a true endurance test — Tour de Cure is designed for participation, not performance.
