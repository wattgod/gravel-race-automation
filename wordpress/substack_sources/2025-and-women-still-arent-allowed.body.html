Between two well-spaced races - Paris-Roubaix and the Tour - she systematically cut weight to prepare for a couple of alpine horror shows (hello, Col de la Madeleine) that make or break a GC in the Tour De France Femmes by leaning on the old school ‘don’t eat anything untl you think you’re going to pass out and then eat a cube of cheese’ diet, or, a professional nutritionist and chef.

From Ferrand-Prévot’s perspective, a woman dieting for performance with professional guidance wasn’t controversial (lol, are you nuts?), but logical, ethical, and defensible, so she made a crucial error when journalists interviewed her about her performances - she told the truth.

The cycling world melted down. Marlen Reusser—Olympic medalist, Tour stage winner, and an actual doctor—said the visible transformation set a dangerous precedent. In her words: “When riders are this successful by becoming so thin, it puts pressure on all of us.”

👏🏻It’s 👏🏻Not 👏🏻 That 👏🏻 Deep 👏🏻 It’s 👏🏻 Sexism

No, I didn’t get a degree in gender studies, and I’m not trying to sleep with a feminist—this is just what this whole thing is mostly about—sexism. While calling it sexism can seem like a throwaway reason, in this instance, it actually is the main reason, and we should say so without hedging or qualification. You can determine this with one simple trick—imagine if this headline featured the male winner of the Tour De France, Pogacar.

“Pogacar methodically lost 4 kg in three months with professional assistance to get ready for the crucial mountain stages in the Tour de France.”

Most people who saw that headline wouldn’t think that was controversial at all, akin to a headline saying ‘cyclist performs lots of intervals in preparation for the high pace when people attack in hopes of dropping rivals’. Strategic dieting, like intervals or any other training intervention, is a normal, established part of a cyclist’s preparation for events.

Why then do we lose our minds when women do what men do? Because in 2025, we still hold women to a different standard than men, and no, that standard is not equal to or higher than men, but worse - the standard we apply to children.

Formally, this is called paternalism, which is basically treating a group of people like a parent would treat a child, because we don’t see them as capable enough to make and take responsibility for their choices.

What’s so slippery about paternalism is that on the surface it comes off as thoughtful and caring - we just want to make sure women put their health at risk -, but buries it’s real offense deeper in the assumption that women, to whom you’re applying more oversight, rules, or consideration, simply aren’t capable or worthy of assuming the choices and responsibilities we’d grant ‘real’ people, like men.

Health ≠ Performance

Whenever I see an athlete or a commentator bleating about high performing athletes ‘Not being healthy,’ a part of me dies.

News flash: the weight and body composition that's best for your health is not the same that's best for your performance.

As counterintuitive as it might seem, the weight/body composition that produces the best blood work numbers, functional fitness (you know, doing real shit other than pedaling), and general life satisfaction/happiness 100% is not the same as the ideal weight/body composition to set a PR on your favorite climb.

In general, your body likes it when you're a bit heavier and dislikes it when you're a bit lighter. And you might ask, 'lighter than what, exactly?' and the answer to that is 'Your set point,‘ or the general weight at which your body hovers.

For example, personally speaking, my 'set point' is roughly 175 lbs. At 'race weight', I can get down to 165 lbs (with pain and suffering). I can also beef up to 190 if, I don't know, I want to appear more manly (also with pain and suffering). But, without really trying, I generally weigh about 175 lbs (I know, that's enormous for a cyclist!)

But, generally, my body prefers to be 190 much more than it likes being 165, probably because 99% of my ancestors spent most of their time trying not to starve to death. Your body probably works the same way.

What's my point? Understand that the body composition that produces the best performances on the bike probably isn't the same body composition that supports health.

Yes, Weight Matters in Cycling

Since we’re already talking about performance, I’m just going to come at the elephant in the room and claim, without a big windup or caveats, that yes, weight matters in cycling performance and, for the most part, being skinny is better than not being skinny.

Every year I listen to very serious people with lots of letters behind their name get onto podcasts and discuss body composition in cycling who grossly downplay the impact of weight on performance and somehow make weight loss out to be evil. I’ll say this again:

Weight matters in cycling performance, and being skinny is generally better than not being skinny.

Look, I don’t like that this is true, but since I respect you as an adult, I’m not going to lie to you and pretend it’s not a thing.

Why does skinny kill? For the same reasons that most NBA basketball players are over 6’7” - because there’s a phenotype that conveys an advantage in a given sport. Specifically, being skinny helps your:

  • Climbing - For every lb you lose, you gain about a second per kilometer uphill. If that sounds modest, understand that losing 5 lbs translates to climbing roughly 30 seconds faster on a short, 5km climb.

  • Acceleration - It is easier to change speeds with less mass to accelerate.

  • Fueling requirements - Less mass is easier to fuel than more mass.

  • Heat Tolerance - Bigger people suffer more in the heat.

  • Aerodynamics - In general, there’s a modest yet significant aerodynamic bump at a lower weight.

Again, everything I just said is only general and can often fall apart in the particular—i.e. There are always exceptions, and those exceptions are often so exceptional that they’re quite visible and thus overrepresented in the media, which misleads many people into thinking the advantages conveyed by phenotype don’t have any physiological underpinnings but arise abitrarily out of a backward, narrow sporting culture.

For example, Muggsy Bogues was 5’3” and had a wildly successful career in the NBA that gave short guys everyone hope but it didn’t change the fact he made the NBA despite being short, not because of it, whereas you could easily argue someone like Shaq was so dominate in the NBA not by virtue of his actually basketball skills (Shaqs free throw percentage was legendarily poor despite being constantly fouled) but because he was over 7’ tall and weighed enough to make The Mountain from game of Thrones feel average.

And so it is with cycling - being skinny generally convenys a performance advantage, so it should be no surprise that the best female cyclists in the world competing in the most prestigous cycling race primarily decided by their climbing ability on 1-2 stages in a well known week or two in late July would dust off their algebra skills, write down their threshold, write down the w/kg value they know they have to be able to sustain to win, and solve for weight.

You Don’t Want What You Think You Want

Why do some people say, “Never meet your heroes”? Because they will disappoint you.

No, you don’t. You don’t want what you think you want. What you really want is a life without tradeoffs, a life where you get all the good things about being an extreme performance outlier and none of the bad things, but that fantasy only exists in the imagination, not reality.
