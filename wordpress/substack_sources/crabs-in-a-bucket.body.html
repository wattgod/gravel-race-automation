After I got my upgrade seven years ago, something happened that I never expected - I became the object of envy.

First, I hope you’ll join me in laughing uncontrollably for several minutes at the thought of my accomplishments causing anyone to feel like I have something worth having. While I’ll concede that it’s not easy to upgrade to Cat 1, it’s hardly the moon landing. While not every cyclist that begins as a CAT 4/5 gets to 1, I think it’s not unreasonable that with 4-5 years of obsession, most people can do enough training crits to get the upgrade coordinator of USAC to click the ‘upgrade’ button grudgingly.

Now, I don’t want to overstate my case here—I only know of one person who ‘hated’ me, and it wasn’t blatant or explicitly verbalized. Nevertheless, it’s worth raising this observation because it says so much about human nature and what you should expect if you do anything well.

With Friends Like These

It’s funny, but a lot of times what’s incriminating isn’t what people say, but what they don’t say.

After I upgraded, my celebrations were sloppier than those of a sorority with a White Claw sponsorship. I would tell anyone who listened that I was a real boy and try to get them to crack a Miller with me. Understandably, many declined because they couldn’t appreciate the superior quality of a fine pilsner from a humble manufacturing town on Lake Michigan. Others, I would learn, rejected my invitation for darker reasons.

One individual was normally friendly, interested, and even encouraging of my racing success, at least until I received my upgrade email. After that, they became curiously remote, cold, and even avoidant.

So did I confront them? Nah! I sleuthed their TrainingPeaks account and, to my astonishment, found a comment confirming what I felt nonverbally.

I’ll admit, this shook me. I was not well-versed in handling negative commentary simply because lower-tier nobodies like myself don’t have anything worth hating. To read that someone who hadn’t even reached my lowly Cat 1 height considered my athletic pedigree to be not just beneath them, but laughable, stung, especially considering that they were a teammate and a friend at that point.

If you gave me money, my friend’s betrayal wouldn’t hurt so much.

After reading this, did I meet them outside their place of work and sucker punch them? Did I find their apartment and show up in a ski mask and inflict thousands of dollars of property damage? Did I sleep with their mother and girlfriend simultaneously while live-streaming to them nonconsenually?

No. I forgave them and never said a damn thing.

Cope (and Seethe)

We don’t like to accept this, but humans have a nature. A constant that’s held forever is that we’re perfectly happy to support, cheer, and celebrate the accomplishments of our peers that are beneath or equal to our own, but the second they exceed our own, we fucking melt down.

Why? Because we don’t want people to do better than us.

The next time someone does something extraordinary (or something fairly pedestrian but not in the bottom quintile), I guarantee you that a healthy chunk of people made aware of the feat will shit all over their accomplishment. While this may seem justified, all criticism of what someone else does ultimately boils down to ‘cope’, which is how people rationalize their own failures or disappointments instead of facing reality.

The funny thing is that the person who did whatever was worth other people hating on, almost certainly didn’t do what they did because they wanted to beat the hater in question, but for their own reasons. Yet, the haters almost always hold up that person’s accomplishments as a mirror to themselves and reach for the same old well well-worn excuses such as:

  • “They got lucky.” When someone does something, it’s never because they worked hard and were talented; it’s just probabilistic fortune.

  • “(Blank) isn’t worth it anyway.” Whatever that person does actually isn’t worth having.

  • “They’re privileged.” Unless you start out as an abandoned baby in the Amazon and raise yourself, anything you were given is a strike against your accomplishment.

  • “They were born different.”

Many forms of coping might be clever and hilarious, but no matter how convincingly a hater disparages someone else's accomplishment, no matter how many people like their crushing comment or hilarious response, it doesn’t change the fact that the hater hasn’t done jack shit and eventually has to confront the fact that they’re a zero.

And since, after a certain point, you can’t continue coping with what someone else does without looking obsessed (and therefore confirming that you care and are envious), all coping gradually gives way to seething, which is silently simmering with anger and resentment. Seething is super healthy and in no way responsible for most of the pathologies of modern life.

Potential = 🍆✊🏻💦

The most compelling thread in all of these different forms of coping is that they seek to separate accomplishment as the lagging outcome of discipline, effort, and fortitude instead of the product of chance.

Why? The former implies that the hater could do it if they applied themselves, and there are few things humans like to accept less than that the person most to blame for who they are now, versus what they could be, is themselves.

Put another way, if you think achievement is primarily determined by luck or privilege, then there’s no impetus to act; if you think achievement is based on discipline and effort, then there is.

Maybe you think I’m going to drone on about how action is hard, and people don’t like doing hard things, all of which is true, but I think the deeper factor in all of this is that if you act, eventually you will be measured. You will see whether your honest efforts produce the outcome you were looking for, or if they don’t, which is horrifying.

None of us wants to give our all only to find out we’re not Taylor Swift, or Michael Jordan, or Messi, but merely ‘respectable yet firmly middling music/athlete enthusiast’. That would mean we’re ordinary. Instead, it’s safer to sit back and judge people from afar without putting ourselves out there.

Haters Really Are Going to Hate

Let my naïvete be a lesson to you - if you ever do anything worth doing, at least one person is going to wish you get T-boned by a cement truck, at least privately. The fact that you have haters confirms that you’ve done something worth having. Welcome it - it means you’ve arrived.

Also, don’t take the hatred of haters personally. No matter how seemingly specific, nasty, targeted, and dirty people can be with their anger, it’s not about you; it’s about them. Your real crime is reminding them of their own inadequacies, which you shouldn’t apologize for any more than you do for breathing. And while I’ll grant that creating a spreadsheet of all the vile things your haters are saying to you and using that as motivation can be a helpful tool, just don’t. Forgive them instead, or, even better, ignore them. Nothing frustrates a hater more than a lack of acknowledgement that they exist.

And most of all? Fucking cherish the people in your life that express genuine joy in your accomplishments without secretly hoping you drown in a bathtub. While there’s no reliable method to determine who these people are objectively, this is a perfect situation to rely on your gut feeling as a guide. If you know people long enough, your lizard brain can usually suss out when someone is being, well, sus after you announce happy news that threatens their status and accomplishments.
