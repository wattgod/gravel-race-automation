When I saw Michael Burleigh roll by, I almost immediately got back into my truck and drove home.

When he saw me, he stopped freewheeling, performed an impossibly tight pirouette on his bike, and put his foot down a few feet from where I was standing.

“Matti Rowe!” he exclaimed. “How the hell are you?”

Instead of saying something normal, I blurted something about having another kid because of a mushroom trip and fearing mortality instead of saying like, I don’t know, something along the lines of ‘How are you doing?’ or ‘How is the family?’ or ‘It sure is hot today.”

But Michael Burleigh isn’t a person to me, he’s El Patron, or, in his words, the Sheriff, more or less the boss of the Front Range peloton from from 2012 to 2017 before he burned out, disappeared, had a few kids, and then pops up every now and then still looking ominously lean and imperious.

But, no offense to Burleigh, that’s not why I was such a stuttering mess looking for any excuse to flake on the race - it was because it was the first race back after my crash.

One Break Breaks Two Things

For those of you who don’t know, which I imagine isn’t very many people, I crashed at Unbound a few months ago and broke a rib. Yes, I’m doing fine. No, you can’t have my address, so please stop sending flowers.

I’ve never broken anything on my body, but since doing so, I’ve realized that when you break something badly enough that you can’t ride for a while, the bone is only the first thing to break—mental breaks always follow.

How is that exactly? Well, the psychlogical contusion would never appear in an x-ray, but maybe it would in a diary entry or the unconscious stream of thoughts in the days and weeks that follow, primarily presenting in ones’s affect or absurd compensatory behaviors that try to delude the athlete into thinking this injury isn’t a setback and is something they can bargain with.

The truth is, however, that whatever fitness and momentum you had before the broken whatever, you’ve lost, and, unlike with other minor injuries, there’s not a 24-hour turnaround and then you’re back on the bike, promisingly pedaling at baseline. You’re in this for weeks, months, and even years, and what that interruption lays bare is how fragile the athletic ego can be and how much it relies on the day-to-day workout performance to steady wavering confidence.

What’s hard to appreciate when you’re healthy is that without nearly daily feedback from a power meter or other external sources of performance, it’s extremely hard to believe in yourself.

I’ll go even further and say one of the reasons that athletes like being athletes is that, especially in endurance training, workouts every day provide the subjective feeling of moving toward something they want which produces a small but powerful jolt of dopamine, serotonin, or whatever other fancy sounding chemicals I can’t be bothered to check underpin motivation every day on par with a 50th anniversary edition Golden tall boy Miller Lite. And just like getting cut off at the bar, once that steady input of rides stops, the buzz wears off, and life returns to its baseline of pointlessness, suffering, and anomie.

The Valley of Despair

Eventually, I started training again. It didn’t start well, and it wasn’t pretty, but it beat not training. As soon as I started, the question that dogged every pedal stroke was ‘How much did you lose?’ or ‘Will you ever be the same?’ or “Is this the beginning of an FTP under 300?’ and on and on.

I should be above looking for answers, but I admit that I couldn’t help but read far too much into every heartbeat, watt, and interval split, and I didn’t like what I saw. Despite my world-class capacity for delusion, there was no question my numbers were down, and I didn’t dare step on a scale and see what had happened to my waistline since launching off of FarmWatts over a month ago, but even the elastic waistline of my sweat pants seemed reluctant to accept me.

Most people crack here. You’re past rock bottom, but still nowhere near what you were. It feels close—only because you’re lying to yourself just enough to keep going—chasing a finish line that keeps sliding away no matter how hard you press.

But none of that really mattered because I was back in the process again, getting rewarded with little hits of dopamine every time I hit the save button on my Garmin after a few hours of riding, adding another bright green square to my TrainingPeaks calendar.

As I rode around my usual roads, I saw construction signs that said, “Summer Vibes Gravel Saturday July __. Expect delays.” The first few times I saw this, I didn’t even entertain signing up. But as I began doing intervals and the rib seemed to handle, at least with a grimace, it’s normal workload without the usual stabbing pain, I found myself lingering on the Gravel Race’s website, entering a few of my personal details in the entry form, only to close the pane right before I was supposed to enter my payment information.

Finally, seemingly against my will (maybe after a couple of Miller Tallboys), I woke up the next day to find a confirmation of race registration in my inbox. I guess I was going to pin a bib whether I wanted to or not.

There’s Always Doubt

Cards on the table, at this point in my career, I have probably paid for over a thousand dollars’ worth of race entry fees I later bailed on. It happens like this:

1. While buzzed or optimistic about my legs, I sign up for a race without thinking clearly about the time commitment, logistics, and expense of it.

2. The race approaches, and all of the above becomes clear.

3. I tell myself that whether I race it or not, I’m not getting my money back, so I should do what I want.

4. I bail.

5. I feel bad I bail.

This time around, I was about fifteen minutes away from number four before I told myself, “You’re a coward.” Suddenly, again against my will, I discovered that I was driving toward the race start with all my shit before I knew what was happening.

If you don’t subscribe I won’t be able to afford to sign up for races I’ll flake on last minute.

Fast forward about an hour, and I was sitting thirty racers deep next to some guy who used to ride for Rio in his early 50s with a puanch unmistakably acquired from one too many IPAs, Zoom meetings, and letting his kids join a travel soccer team. He looked me up and down and said, “I’m just hoping to finish strong. It looks like you’re hoping to do the same. Wanna work together?”

That was rock bottom.

The Leap In the Dark

The gun went off, and the neutral dragged on for miles, and I kept drifting away from the front through my unparalleled ability to surrender advantageous positions in a pack through unnecessary braking and cowardice. Finally the moto guy honked ominously and a bunch of eager, veiny legged hyenas at the front charged into the gap he left and attacked while I was about twenty wheels back, but thankfully I didn’t have to try at all because the attackers were foiled by a 90 degree turn in the road which they cut gingerly with squeeling disc brakes.

Over the next twenty minutes or so, a few fellahs threw in some jabs here and there, but no one really dug in the knife until 55th avenue, onto the final miles of the Boulder Roubaix course where a guy I noticed before the start that was running what looked like 28s (I was running Vittoria Mezcals!) launched an attack so hard up a longer kicker I thought his chamois was going to split.

This was the moment I had been fearing for the last six weeks that I wasn’t sure I would be able to meet. No what my power meter had told me, no matter what my scale said, no matter how high my CTL was, no matter how my heart rate decoupled, at the root of my anxiety was this fear that all of that shit I rely on to guide and measure myself were all just made up, a bunch of random numbers and fake metrics that would be laid bare by the violence of some scrappy cowboy running 28 mm tires on mtb tracks.

But then he died a bit. My legs blurred into a 120 cadence, and with a seemingly effortless 700-watt acceleration, I easily glided up to him and slotted into the very last spot in the front group behind the Sheriff, whom I dared not pass.

Oh my fucking God, I cannot tell you how relieved I was after the one acceleration. My power meter WASN’T a liar, like Caroline, my ex from Duluth; she was true, or at least a better liar. As the relief washed over me other attacks rained down but I felt like a character in a video game with a seemingly endless store of turbo boosts and simply bridged up to attacks past conga lines of other rides that had run out of gears and suddenly was in a break away up Left Hand Canyon of two other guys: Michael Burleigh and Ryan Cross.

You Can’t Take Ls Forever

They both beat me. Why doesn’t matter. We don’t have to get into my personal failures and drag on this essay into a short novel. They may have casually dropped me on Rowena and disappeared until the finish, but the neat thing about a podium is that there are three spots, so no matter how much they dusted me, I still rolled across the line and claimed the third step.

And you know who greeted me after the fact? My son and mother-in-law. Hot tip: If you’re going to do a bike race when your mother-in-law is in town, I highly recommend placing well so she walks around to various vendors at the after-party and introduces herself as the mother-in-law of the guy who placed 3rd overall 😂.

Look, it’s been a tough 12 months. In the last year:

1. I Got COVID.

2. Then got a lung infection that kept me off the bike for 2 months.

3. Then quit nicotine.

4. Then got depressed.

5. Then gained 30 lbs.

6. Then, I panicked and dieted and trained to get in shape for Unbound.

7. Then crashed and broke my rib 5 miles in.

I needed a goddamn W, man.

I know it’s just third place at a local gravel race, but standing on the podium next to Michael Burleigh (omg omg omg!) in a brand new Jean jacket with my son looking up at me while my mother-in-law kept handing me Coors lites made the seemingly endless succession of Ls worth it.

Oh, and I’m Not the Only One

Dylan Johnson, a fractionally less handsome, aerobically gifted, and well known gravel cyclist, recently made his comeback after getting T-boned by a truck (while he was on a bike, not in a car) at the Leadville 100, a race I turned down an invitation to despite the organizers begging me to challenge Keegan (I can barely get on a MTB and have been rejected every time I applied).

What was that like emotionally? Well, it’s not the most masculine thing I’ve ever seen but Dylan did go on social media and eloquently blast authentic vulnerability all over the Gram, removing any doubt that even if you’re gravel famous and have known nothing but success, there’s every chance the devil will find you in your darkest hour and confirm your worst fears, that you’ll never get back to where you were and you’ll have to go back to working as a Subway waiter instead of signing the blouses of gravel groupies.

Share this with someone that needs to feel better about themselves chances are they won’t suck forever either.
