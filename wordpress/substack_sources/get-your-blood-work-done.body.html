You Should Get Your Labs Done

Why did I put myself through all that? Because every year, I get bloodwork done after each cycling season.

Why get your bloodwork done? Because when you’re not responding to your training well, or you’re getting sick a lot, or your body is acting weird, or you start feeling funny, it’s much, much better to have a baseline of biometric data year over year to draw upon to convince yourself that something isn’t fundamentally broken in you. And also because one way to avoid dying early from something completely preventable is to get appropriate screening so you’ll notice early if some biomarker starts heading south.

Every year I do this for myself, and every year, if I put this recommendation on a Post-it note for an athlete, I'll not-so-subtly ask my athletes to do the same. Along with taking a season break, reviewing the previous year, and setting SMARTY goals for the next year, reviewing your health and getting appropriate bloodwork done is common-sense, 101 shit athletes should do to start the next year on the right foot.

Of course, many athletes don’t do this because they’re athletes, and if you do athlete things, that means you’re always perfectly healthy, and there’s no chance you could be rotting on the inside even though you look and perform great on the outside.

I’m Sorta Dying

Despite passing out before I could get my phlebotomist all the required vials, I had given enough that overnight my blood got processed and the results got uploaded to a data center in your backyard that you hate, and my phone vibrated with a little notification. I opened the app. My results were a bloodbath.

That’s right—despite being an ‘athlete,’ despite training 750+ hours a year for the past ten years, the organ I’ve probably stressed the most, my heart, apparently isn't getting the protection I thought all that pedaling bought me.

The number of cholesterol-carrying particles moving through my arteries—measured by ApoB and LDL particle count—is way, way over target. My ApoB is 112 mg/dL, and my LDL particle count is 1,898 nmol/L. Given their numbers, I’ve probably got more of these little bastards circulating around than I should, increasing the chances that some will eventually end up embedded in my artery walls and form plaques perfect for heart attacks and strokes. My clean-up crew—large HDL particles, the guys I hired to help haul cholesterol away—is also slightly below the lab’s reference range, even though my overall HDL cholesterol is high. Taken together, my blood isn’t exactly the clear, healthy stuff in a medical textbook, but more like the approximate viscosity of Miller Lite.

So how is it possible that an Elite athlete (I got rejected from the Elite wave at Unbound this year) with 7% body fat (I’m at least 15% but maybe 20%) and a VO2 max of 75 ml/kg (according to my Garmin (lol), where I underreport my body weight by 15 lbs) has the cholesterol values of a sedentary, nursing-home bacon enthusiast?

Because I won the genetic lottery for all the wrong reasons.

I got genetic testing a few years ago, and while it didn’t quite prove that Chris Hemsworth, the Australian heartthrob, and I are related, it did uncover one unbelievable stroke of bad luck—we both have APOE ε4/ε4 homozygosity, which basically means there’s a very high chance we develop early onset Alzheimers and die after having forgotten everything that mattered to us and everyone we ever loved.*

Do you want my kids to grow up without a father? Your subscription supports my recovery from the cholesterol from years of abusing dairy products.

Why?

If you have that rare (2% of humanity) variant, you suck at handling cholesterol-rich lipoproteins, especially if your diet contains a lot of saturated fats from dairy products, delicious, fatty meats, and other food items God made to keep us happy that consist of 99% of my diet besides Miller Lite, which I now realize I need to give up almost entirely so I don’t start forgetting people’s names before I hit my 40s, because, unfortunately, no matter how much I pedal, it’s not reversing the effects of criminal saturated fat intake combined with a genetic predisposition to not clear it.

Athletic ≠ Healthy

You might think one bad blood test isn’t cause to make sudden, drastic diet changes and give up things like hickory-smoked maple bacon served over a bed of butter-soaked home-fried potatoes nestled next to a pie-sized cheesy ham omelet, but my lifestyle change isn’t happening because of one blood test. It’s happening because of several blood tests, and they’ve consistently shown that my blood carries increasingly large amounts of cholesterol.

That’s the thing. If it was just one test, I could brush it off as an anomaly. But I’ve had several blood tests over the years that all point to the same issues and a worrying trend that, if I don’t address it, could lead to some ugly consequences down the line, all because I didn’t want to give up one of the things I love most in the world—dairy.

And look, I’m not saying that you have this issue—you’re probably part of the 98% of people who don’t have this particular genetic variant—but it’s worth investing a little money in getting appropriate bloodwork and establishing useful baseline values as a sanity check for your health. It’s not particularly cheap, the process is annoying, and occasionally you’ll get news that may require annoying life changes, but it sure as hell beats assuming everything is great, and you can eat, drink, smoke, and huff whatever you want just because you do a lot of cardio.
