Microstory

"Does part of you wish you were racing today?” My teammate’s cousin said.

Lololololol F**k no. I was standing on the sideline of the race track watching the circuit race play out, and every single time the peloton rolled through, I felt waves of relief wash over me, like I was sipping an ice-cold Miller after a hard day typing on the computer.

I cracked HARD in the queen stage yesterday. When someone is pumped, when they’re excited to compete and fight, you can say, “They’ve got that dog in them.” Well, my dog died, and the only thing that could have revived it would be several lines of blow and a gun to my head.

But I didn’t need any stimmies to sit on the sidelines and completely mislead Alex’s family with my race commentary. I enjoyed trying to convince his relatives that riding at the back of the peloton was just part of Cole’s plan to win the sprint, that Henry’s least favorite type of bike racing is crits (it’s all he lives for), and if I were out there, I’d have lapped the field twice already.

In the end, Luke, a young gun who was just guest riding (meaning he’s not a real Rio rider that involves a lot of blind foldes and leather), got our best result of the week with an 8th place finish in the sprint. We would have offered him a contract then and there for next year, but immediately after crossing the line he rolled up to sniff the chamois of his idle, some guy with a last name of Boardman, and ran into our other guest rider and crashed, splitting his knee open and embarrassing himself and team Rio so badly that he quit cycling and we pretended not to know him.

Shoot Me (What sucked today)

“I’ll see you again soon,” he said, and then he hugged me.

No, you won’t, I thought. We’ll finish this hug, you’ll walk through that door, and I will never see you again for the rest of my life.

This doesn’t get to me. I’m not choked up; it’s just the truth.

Cycling is a weird sport. While bike racing, you end up sharing a level of intimacy with your teammates second only to what happens in the bedroom. Not only do we get to see each other at our best (and our worst), but we also get to witness each other doing the most rare and vulnerable of things - trying our best. When racing, we’re not hiding behind carefully curated social media accounts or irony; we’re giving everything we’ve got to get the best result possible, and if we fall short, it’s not because we were holding back, but because we weren’t good enough.

Oddly, most of these people are strangers. You might say that your family, close friends, and significant others understand you best, but that’s just not true - these strangers do.

For example, sure, you love your partner, but your partner doesn’t know what it’s like to train for and race at a National level. Even if they did, they, unlike your teammates, don’t see your character revealed in the way you ride, suffer, fight, risk, win, and most of all, lose, and that’s just on the bike. There are also all the moments in between the racing, traveling, cooking, cleaning, and most of all, waiting that reveal even more about you. In a funny way, your teammates see sides of you you haven’t even seen clearly yourself.

And yet, as soon as the race ends, our lives unwind even faster than they came together for the race, and we all split, never to see each other again.

I’ve been doing this shit for so long that I’ve forgotten everyone I’ve known through bike racing. Sometimes I’ll be bullshitting with another Rio Grande lifer who might bring up a former teammate I hadn’t thought of in years that rage-quit the sport, moved from the Caribbean to the UK, and became a music-playing goth and hasn’t looked back since.

You remember quickly connecting with them, feeling the first few embers of a new friendship ignite, but they have long since disappeared into the world, and even if you did reach out, they would already have forgotten your name.

I understand why Svein Tuft tattooed “We will never be here again” on his arm—in cycling, nothing lasts, especially teammates. And in many ways, especially for teammates like Cole, it’s a tremendous relief.

Pretty Neat

If you’ve ever gone out to a restaurant and split an appetizer with a couple of people, you know how much of a nightmare it can be to make sure that everyone leaves dinner without feeling chaffed.

Now imagine a six-day stage race with flights, rental cards, Airbnbs, and food. It really is a nightmare.

Thank God for Splitwise (not an affiliate of Gravel God LLC). Splitwise makes it easy to add receipts and split expenses, so you’re not left holding the bag while your teammates coast on your credit card generosity and pretend they’ll pay you back when their next paycheck clears.

My favorite feature is being able to invoice people for nontraditional expenses like emotional labor or bespoke chores you did repeatedly without so much as thanks, like constantly cleaning up after someone or preventing them from having an emotional breakdown.

If you find yourself traveling with a bunch of dirtbag low-life cyclists, Splitwise is essential to keeping your money, and most of all, your sanity.
