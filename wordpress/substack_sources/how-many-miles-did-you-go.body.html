"How many miles did you go"

"A couple," I answered.

"No, but how many?"

"Eh, couldn't say, "I said. "I wasn't keeping track."

That's a lie—of course, I'm keeping track. If it’s not on Strava, it didn’t happen, but I lie anyway. Anyone who asks me ‘how many miles I've gone’ doesn’t know anything about riding bikes.

How many miles have I gone? That’s not how you ask someone about their ride, not someone who races bikes anyway.

"But how many miles tho?"

"Couldn't say, sorry! Like I said, I didn't record anything."

It’s funny, but I find that no matter how much I refuse to answer that question, most people who ask that question won’t stop asking you until you tell them, especially if that person happens to be my Father. He does this charming exercise where he'll try to narrow down what you did by asking at the extremes:

"How Long did you ride?"

"Idk. Didn't track it."

"Zero miles?"

"Well no."

"200 miles?"

"Well no."

And so forth until he gets within about five miles of what I actually rode. 🙄

Mercifully, that ends the conversation, but I'm sure this all might seem confusing. Why is it so hard for me to tell someone that I rode precisely 38 miles with 3790 feet of elevation gain in two hours?

Because I don't want to give them a number that allows them to judge me, to quantify what I did with something they can relate to.

For example, if I said '40 miles,' that wouldn’t just be a normal lie (remember I rode less), but a humiliating lie because rounding up in my favor, even to say 50 miles, will not meaningfully change how my bike ride mileage is judged because the closest reference point the average person has for the time it takes to travel a distance is by imagining driving the same distance in a car, which makes even the fastest cyclists appear lumbering.

People ballpark car trips at highway speeds, which are roughly four times faster than the speed most cyclists can sustain—hardly a fair standard by which to judge a rider's distance covered.

And what sympathies does a driver share with a cyclist who encounters environmental factors like wind, temperature, hills, etc., all of which dramatically reduce a cyclist’s speed? Zero. A driver’s speed wouldn’t waver if it encountered conditions that would slow most cyclists to a pathetic crawl.

Using driving mileage as the most accessible way for the average person to understand how tough a cyclist's ride was might be understandable, but it almost always trivializes those efforts in comparison to a car, contextualizing all but the most outrageous rides to a Normie as sort of weak.

Eventually, these exchanges deflated me. I often felt almost insulted by people seeming unimpressed or bored by my ride just because they could travel the same distance by pushing down a pedal for an hour while they kept it between the ditches, so I began minimizing and obfuscating my rides before their question did while slowly becoming bitter than I had to do so.

I used to really resent that people asked when they clearly never bothered to understand, but for a while now, I've stopped feeling that way. Let’s pretend it’s because I’m getting older and wiser (it’s the 🍄s! ).

I realized that my desire to explain to them what to ask about so they could understand the physical and mental toll of the ride I'd just done on its own merits and place it in the broader context of my training, process objectives, and primary goals is not about them, it’s about me. I mean, of course, it is - obviously. I’m embarrassed it took me so long to see that, but there are far sharper tools in the shed.

It struck me that someone happy with their ride, because it met their own standard, would never care if someone else did; they would only seek external validation if they were insecure.

I think that realization is valid, but I also think a tempting conclusion to make immediately after that epiphany is that it’s right to continue being evasive when someone asks me how many miles I’ve ridden because I don’t need their attaboys to feel affirmed.

The problem with an evasive response is that you might not want to be evasive - you might be proud of your ride and want to tell people - and so playing off your ride and pretending it was nothing isn’t just deceitful, but a self-betrayal.

Remember, I realized that if a ride met my own standards, I should be happy with it. Acting like I wasn’t because I didn’t want to be misunderstood by a stranger is still the actions of someone too invested in the stranger.
