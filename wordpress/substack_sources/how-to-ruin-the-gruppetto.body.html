The Rad Gravel race in Trinidad, Colorado, starts violently and quickly breaks up before leaving town. Its sharp, steep hills immediately provide the selections that usually take hours to form in a normal gravel race.

Twenty minutes in, I found myself in the third chase group on the road with about seven riders working hard until it became clear that we weren’t going to catch up to the second group. That’s when one of two white-kitted Rule 28 riders suddenly started talking a lot.

“That’s it, boys.” He said. “Nice and smooth now.”

OMFG, shoot me. Naturally, the twenty-pound-overweight, white Rule 28 kit-wearing, grey-haired, ostrich-calved, MTB tire-shredding guy has elected himself captain of our Group and provides unsolicited guidance on how to pace the climbs.

“On these climbs, I’m just going to park it at 330,” he said. “No need to dip into threshold all the time.”

You Are Not ‘the Boss.’ You’re In the 3rd Group.

There’s a special place in hell for self-appointed ‘road captains’ of small groups of riders far behind the leaders who spend a lot of time telling everyone else how to ride.

I first encountered this a long, long time ago in the now-defunct Steamboat Stage Race in the CAT 4s. One of the top five riders patrolling the front for his better-placed teammate loudly complained when riders from other teams attacked on downhills or in unlikely places.

“So stupid!!!” the rider would say, shaking their head. “This is literally the worst place to use your watts.”

Now, what I’m about to say might hurt the delicate sensibilities of riders in introductory categories of bike racing, but here it is as politely as I can say it:

If you’re a Cat 4, shut the fuck up about your watts.

You’re NOT good - you’re A CAT 4. When you comment on the best time to use watts in a race, you’re indirectly implying that you’re smart, but that’s patently false - YOU’RE A CAT 4. If you were so smart, you wouldn’t be racing in this category.

What is this exactly? It’s a short guy-syndrome, but it's on the bike. It’s slow, never will bes (like myself) who fail to recognize that they’re not bossing the front of the bunch like Lance Armstrong in his pomp, but pack fodder that the leaders never even saw on their pee break.

Over a decade later, I see the same shitting playing out in the second and (and sometimes third, 😬) Group on the road, guys with the best shit - the skin suit, the Dylan Johnson-approved tires, the flat plug tucked in the sleeve, aero socks, carefully calculated carbohydrate products from approved brands tucked into jersey pockets - telling everyone what to do while carrying on in this cloying, avuncular tone, and it makes me want to shoulder-check them into a cattle guard at 40 miles per hour.

If you subscribe, I’ll try to pay the Rule 28 guys to stop racing.

What Exactly Do I Want to Say to Them?

First of all, cut the shit with the ‘cool guy’ routine. Stop pretending your ‘big plan’ for the day was to get shelled by the leaders (AND the second Group on the road, JFC, you’re slow), and that all of those IDIOTS ahead of us somehow aren’t pacing correctly. We’ll inevitably reel them in when they explode, as if it’s some sort of mistake that they’re as far ahead as they are.

Newsflash - they’re ahead of us because they’re better than we are, not because they made a mistake.

Second, I am not your teammate. Stop telling me how to ride in the Group. This is gravel - it’s anarchy. I’m under no obligation to ride with you in any cooperative capacity at all.

Bike racing is cutthroat, and I will work with you to the extent that it benefits me, not because I signed up to be the helper bitch of some complete stranger for 110 miles.

See, here’s the thing - this shit bag climbed like an anchor but descended like a demon, so guess how he recommended the entire group pace themselves up the climb? Chill, naturally. This is when chatty scum bags always say things like, “stay smooth,” which just means - don’t go too hard and pop me, this isn’t what I’m good at.

Do you know what he was good at, though? Dive bombing downhill into blind corners. He had zero issue doing that, and the strangest thing happened when he did - he didn’t say, “Hey guys, take it slow, no sense crashing out here.” Lol, of course not, this was his strength, his jam, his shit, and he smashed the downhills and didn’t think twice about chopping people’s wheels while doing so.

These prima donnas are always the same, always telling the Group to ride in a way that perfectly suits their strengths and weaknesses under the pretense of ‘riding smart,’ when in reality they’re just trying to gas light everyone else into thinking, for example, that you’re somehow morally wrong for riding up a hill hard becasue, “that’s not a good use of your watts,” even though that’s literally the BEST time to gain the most time from your watts.

Another thing with the talking - don’t. This isn’t a coffee ride; this is a race. If you’re so comfortable with the pace that you frequently find that you can (and want to) hold conversations with your teammates or other people, get the F out of the Group - try harder. It’s unbelievably demeaning to be suffering in the wheels. At the same time, some sandbagging goober rolls up and down the pace line having nonsense conversations about how he trained too hard this week and was tired or blah blah blah. It’s like, dude, if you can hold a conversation, you’re not trying hard enough. Shut up or pedal harder, you’re not at a hair salon.

Lastly, they lose it if you’re in the Group and decline to ride in a way the Boss approves. White kit Rule 28 guy (and his equally annoying teammate) became increasingly incensed when some Pas Normal guy frequently surged hard on uphills but gave garbage and inconsistent pulls on flats and downhills. At one point, the Pas Normal guy came to the front of the paceline, gave the most limp, anemic pull, and then dropped off the front back to the shelter of the draft. As he got to the back, one of the two Rule 28 guys tore into him, calling him every name in the book.

Look, if you’re invested in the ideal pace line or group cohesion, I can see how that would be frustrating, but it is what it is. This kid wasn’t on their team or anyone else’s team in the Group and could ride however he wanted.

Here’s an old rule of thumb from road racing - if you don’t like how someone is riding in a group, you have three options:

  1. Yell.

  2. Seethe.

  3. Drop them.

That’s the thing—no matter how fast the Rule 28 guys thought they were, they were in the third Group. They were in the third Group, not because it was all part of a grand strategy to claw back forty-five minutes in the last twenty miles, but because they didn’t have the legs to hang with faster riders.

The same was true for the kid they yelled at. If you have beef with someone not taking pulls or screwing up the pace line, the solution is straightforward, get in front of them when they’re on the end, let a gap to the Group open up in front of you until you’re both dangling ten seconds or so off the back, and then put the hammer down and drop them; if you can’t do that then keep your mouth shut.

Did I say any of this? Nah. I kept my mouth shut. The only thing more unnerving than someone openly confronting someone else in a bottom-feeder group is acting like the Boss doesn’t exist.

They say something? Don’t respond.

They ask a question? Ignore.

They look at you? Pretend something is in the distance.

As the course hit the first of many long climbs, the kid they yelled at got popped, and I found myself uncomfortably trading pulls with a couple of other guys and the white kitted Rule 28 morons. Because I’m a terrible bike racer, I could feel that I couldn’t sustain this pace for another 70 miles. Instead of doggedly hanging onto the Group as long as possible, I simply let the wheel go before me and watched them disappear into the distance.

Finally, I was alone and no longer had to deal with their condescending BS.

Sometimes, Justice is Served

Or so I thought.

After spectacularly exploding and limping up several long, high elevation pitches at a pace that wouldn’t threaten a lawnmower, the kid the mouth breathers had yelled at rode past me, said “good job” (Which I hate. ‘Good job?’ If I were doing a good job I wouldn’t be 30 minutes off the back half way into the race red lining in Zone 1, you dick), and then abruptly stopped making good progress and dangled about twenty seconds in front of me for an hour. It seemed everyone in the 3rd Group was some flavor of intolerable idiot.

Shocking, as we climbed, we rounded a corner and saw a white dot appear in the distance. NO FING WAY!! One of the Rule 28 guys had exploded and was going nowhere fast. As he drew closer, I wondered if it was the guy who had yelled at the kid who was about to overtake him.

IT WAS! The kid who can’t ride a pace line to save his life rode past the white-kitted-Rule 28 guy that had chewed him out, said something to him (probably ‘Good Job’), and then proceeded to put in a dig.

I’ve worked long, hard days of punishing physical labor and finished it getting a back massage from my girlfriend while I ate a steak. I found $200 on a ride once. A drunk sorority girl on Pearl Street once commented on the muscularity of my calves as I rode by. My wife once thanked me for being thoughtful. None of those experiences combined was even remotely as satisfying as watching the Rule 28 doucebag get dropped by the kid he yelled at for not riding correctly in the paceline.

What’s that MLK quote, “The arc of history is long and bends toward justice?” Sure, let’s go with that.

Well, I have to say, a few moments in bike racing this year have been more satisfying than seeing this self-important windbag immediately get hoisted on his own petard in such dramatic fashion so quickly after he’d managed to embody the worst aspects of bike racing all at once. While the part of me hopes that getting absolutely karmically annihilated will be a life-changing event for this clown, maybe even convincing him to change to a different endurance sport where he won’t be recognized, I don’t get the sense that the guy does a lot of journaling, so there’s every chance that other unfortunates will have to endure his toxic prescence in the third or fourth chase group of any gravel race he enter.

Ride Hard, Quietly

It’s a shame. If you get shelled and end up in a group of riders far, far off the lead, instead of pretending it was your plan and telling everyone else what to do, just ride hard, quietly.

Ride hard? You owe it to yourself to give your best effort and finish the race for all the time you put into training and racing. To pretend it’s some hobby that you don’t care about, that’s not worthy of your best.

Ride quietly? Everyone else who you’re riding with worked hard to be there and paid lots of money to be here, too. They didn’t sign up to pull you around or listen to you talk for five hours, or god forbid, get yelled at.
