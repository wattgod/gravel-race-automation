I could see Chevez in the distance over the gravel seas, a once small, white kitted spec gradually drawing closer and closer as I hammered to catch up with him, but I never made contact again. Just as I finally got within one roller of him, my unsettling, barely inflated rear tire finally stopped pretending to roll and flatted to the rim and took the full weight of my hulking Norwegian frame with a sickening screech. I put my foot down, whipped out my inflator, and frantically blasted air into the rear tire. My tire responded less than Keeley did to my letters after we broke up a few miles from here seventeen years ago. The wheel wouldn't be rolling anytime soon.

I flipped through my Garmin and brought up the race route, my eyes scanning the checkpoint list. Then I saw it—a 102-mile aid station, 10.5 miles away.

Assuming I could average 3 miles an hour walking, ten and a half miles would take me just over three hours to complete. If I did that, I'd probably need another three hours to knock out the final fifty miles, so if I finished, I'd be staring at an 11-hour day, the equivalent of a slow Unbound time, except that I was only riding 150 miles, not 200.

I looked around me to see if a farmhouse was nearby to get help, but there was nothing but rolling hills of corn pierced by one improbably gravel road as far as I could see.

And that's how I ended up walking at Gravel Worlds 2025.

Bike Racing is Not for Pussies

I didn't know this was possible, but Gravel Worlds found a way to make riding in a gravel peloton even more terrifying by making us start in the dark. So, in addition to the usual butt puckering I do when starting a national level gravel race where 70+ monsters with the lowest VO2max in the low 80s (they'll allow in a 62 if you give the organizer a foot massage, ask me how I know) all fighting for at most two clear riding lanes at ~30 mph, we added the additional variables of darkness, heavy fog that refused to leave after thunderstorms the night before, and somehow an incredible amount of dust despite the recent rainfall that reduced visibility down to, if you were lucky, a red rear taillight about 5 feet in front of your face.

But I wasn't scared. Unlike every other gravel race I've shown up for, my courage and handling skills kept me easily in the top 10 wheels without putting my nose in the wind, and by the time we got to the first mud section, I had barely burned a match while all other scrubs behind me had gotten dropped because they were too pussy to ball with the real gravel ballers.

Just kidding - I instantly got dropped. I may have been the second person to get shelled out of the pro field, only blowing by some hapless tool that couldn't summon 500 watts for 70 seconds required to hand on to one of the first few rollers on the course.

But even as I slid past this weak, unlovable athlete who will never pass on their genes, by the time we got to the next roller, it was my turn to get blown out the back, watching the drone-like swarm of red lights form into a conga line over the downhill before lurching beyond my reach with horrible speed.

And that was that - before I even got ten miles in, my race was over, and if I'm honest, no matter how many times I played it (then at least) I think the same shit would have happened. Honestly, that one time I went to Meow Wolf on psychedelic drugs and thought a tree stump was trying to eat me was less disturbing than trying to override my survival impulse and stay in the soupy murk of the peloton.

And so began what I knew this race would always be - a 150-ish mile death march.

Ignorance Really is Bliss

I wasn't the only one to feel this way, and I have receipts to prove it, but I don't care to justify my cowardice through group consensus - I just don't want to be a coward.

Since I've had a lot of time to reflect on what a joke I am as a person, a husband, employee, father, er, I mean bike racer, I think the real issue with the terror of gravel isn't that it's terrifying - even though it is- it's that the terror is not worth focusing on. It's something to compartmentalize and forget, and I realized this driving home going west on I-80 at 84 mph while passing 18-wheelers.

Huh? Well, the interstate wasn't really built for tourists; it was built for commerce, which means that every day, tens of thousands of 18-wheelers rumble down the highway in both directions in the right lane, 5 mph under the speed limit, forcing the rest of them to pass. The average person doesn't think twice about this, but if they read any Reddit threads about what truck drivers wish normie drivers knew, it's that trucks are dangerous as hell, 50,000 lb+ unstoppable death machines that will win in any accident 10/10 times versus any other vehicle on the road (other than another semi). If you were sane, when you're driving, you should overtake them as quickly as possible and stay the hell away from them in case one of their wheels blows off, a strong wind shoves you into their side, or god forbid the truck driver accidentally steers funny at the end of their monster shift. I bet, statistically, overtaking a semi on the highway is one of the most dangerous things most people ever do.

But no one thinks about this, because despite the inherent dangers and probabilities, the average person is blissfully unaware of this and doesn't death grip their steering wheel 100 yards behind a semi, they just pass the damn thing in the left lane and nothing bad happens (unless it does and everyone in the vehicle dies).

All of life is like this. It is beset with danger that we're mostly unaware of or indifferent toward unless we have a complex and then begin to fixate on one of the least likely, worst outcomes happening, which is exactly what I visualize happening whenever I'm in a peloton in a gravel race. My default assumption is that there'll be a massive high-speed pileup with snapped carbon bikes and ruptured internal organs, and while that absolutely will happen, it probably won’t happen here, on this day, to me.

But until I can stop imagining the semi truck tires exploding as I pass them in the right lane, I'll keep white-knuckling 20 feet off the back of the last person in the peloton, imagining everyone is about to crash at 30 mph.

If you don’t subscribe, I’ll have to go back to working the pole, and those older women don’t tip for sh*t.

"You didn't bring a tube?"

Chevez might be from Canada, but even his Canadian niceness struggled to hide his tone when he realized I hadn't packed a few basic repair supplies, like a tube just in case, you know, you catastrophically flat and your tubeless setup lets you down in the middle of nowhere without support or hope.

"Take mine," he said, and he didn't just give me the tube but also helped me install it because my bitch fingers couldn't pry the Pathfinder Pro 47mm tires off the rim.

I felt bad. While Chevez and I had ridden together since I caught him at the first mud section, where both of us had the pleasure of getting absolutely rinsed by a visibly pissed Peter Stetina, who had been furiously clearing peanut butter mud out of his rear fork while yelling to no one, I have to admit I was bummed he was helping me out so much. First off I didn't want to ruin his race and hold him up so long, but secondly if I was going to have a terrible day - and I was definitely going to have a terrible day- I'd rather do it alone where I'm free to be as negative as I want to be without having to be artificially cheerful so I didn't put off my gruppetto comrade and ensure he never ate at a Rio Grande Mexican restaurant.

But now I had to put on an act for at least the next four hours instead of getting to call myself stupid in between random yelling into the void. Worse, after we got my tire inflated (after somehow getting the spoke and cush core out), I had to stop every 20 minutes to reinflate the tube, AND he also stopped! Jesus Christ, I had never met a less cutthroat bike racer in my life. If I wasn't careful, I'd have to send him a Christmas card.

But everything has limits, even the kindness of Canadians, and when I stopped to inflate a third time, he sort of edged away as if I stank. I pounced on the moment and told him to keep going and not worry about me—I'd maybe catch him later.

Finally, I was alone and fucked, the way gravel racing is meant to be.

After I crossed the line and uncrossed my eyes, I found my phone and checked in with several people who had texted to see if I was alive.

After I responded to a few, one friend said, "I'm impressed by the way you suffered out there."

But I wasn't suffering tho.

There's this space inside that Gravel changes. I don't know what to call it, but it's the same thing that allows me not to pick up my phone during dinner or want my intervals to end. Even when I was walking in the middle of nowhere, I didn't feel anxious, just centered, and if anything, the fact that I didn't feel anxious almost made me nervous because I don't want to turn into some sort of Buddhist nutjob that never overreacts to things. I walked three miles an hour while pushing my bike in what felt like bowling shoes, ten miles out from help under a blazing hot sun, an hour after draining the last drop from my water bottle, without a phone to contact emergency services. I was indifferent, arguably euphoric, and I had no idea why.

Gravel racing is supposed to be an adventure. Many people have forgotten that, but it's no mistake that the race distances are so long and the roads sometimes become muddy ditches—it's not supposed to end fast.

Look, maybe I'm alone in this, but I spend a lot of my life trying to get to things. Sometimes, it's the end of a meeting, or maybe after waking up, I just can't wait until I get to sleep again. Whatever it is, there's a lie at the bottom of it that I've stared in the face many times but can't shake, and that's something like "when I get to ___, then I'll be ___".

But no matter how many times I've gotten to ___, whatever ___ I think I'll find there either doesn't last or isn't nearly as satisfying as it's anticipation turned out to be.

I think, and this is embarrassing to admit, but for the first time, somewhere out there to the East of Lincoln, I finally started enjoying racing for the right reasons. It wasn't turning the pedals hard because I wanted a good finish (that was long gone), or because it would get me laid (I have three kids! (Or will soon!)), or because I had something to prove to anyone - it just felt good to bury myself for no other reason than it felt good to want the moment I was in.

Like, when is the feeling you're in ever going to be enough?

After I had walked for 45 minutes and seen, with some bitterness, age grouper after age grouper blow by me without even asking if I was OK, some handsome, tattooed king stopped and said, "You want a tube?"

Hell yes, I wanted a tube. My next OnlyFans subscription might be a livestream of cycling tubes under a tasteful down-light at different angles with soft jazz playing in the background. He gave me that tube, told me good luck, and left me in the side of the road to try to jam this shiny pink condom tube of unknown size in between the tire and the rim without accidentally pinching it when I inflated it, assuming that my Silica Battery Powered Inflator pump that I had already used for at least 4 separate inflations earlier today wouldn't suddenly rin out of battery and leave me begging for someone else to stop because of course I hadn't brought a hand pump it CO2 cartridges because I was running Enve wheels with bulletproof 47 mm Pathfinders Pro tires which was a little like commutting around in an Abraham's tank. Theoretically, it was impossible for me to flat.

As I screwed on the Silica inflator to the tube, I mashed the button on the device and braced myself for nothing to happen- instead, two miracles happened simultaneously.

First, the tube inflated fully. Second, an absurd desire to jam to the finish line as fast as possible, even though I would still finish DFL in the pro field, filled my heart. I began riding hard for its own sake, because it honored the race and myself.

A few hours later, I crossed the line. The announcer seemed embarrassed to say my name, and mothers shielded their children's eyes as I walked by because I rode so slowly. The guy handing out hot dogs after the race almost refused to get me one on principle until my teammate, who had finished a respectable 27th place several hours earlier and had already showered, eaten, called his girlfriend, and volunteered at a homeless shelter, intervened and threatened to film him.

Unless he gave me a hot dog, so he relented.

After talking to Chevez, I bought hundreds of dollars' worth of schwag and two Miller Lites. I sat at a table alone, swatting flies after I promised my teammate that "I'd be back in a minute," letting the fine American Pilsner wash over me while I made uncomfortably long eye contact with complete strangers.

I love this shit, man. I can't wait for next year.
