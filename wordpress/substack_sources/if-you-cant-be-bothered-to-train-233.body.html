If you haven't started supplementing your workouts with carbs and caffeine by this point, do us both a favor and stop reading my shit - I can't reach you and, absent a night of 🍄, no one else will either.

So, what supplement is left that I haven't mentioned? Well, something I haven't touched on would result in charges of coaching criminal negligence if I didn't mention it—sleep.

Sleep?

Yes, sleep.

Sleep fits in perfectly with my current working definition of supplements - something that completes something else; it's just overlooked because most people typically don't see sleep as something necessary to adapt to training - until they overdo it and their body goes to shit - but mostly see sleep as a burden, a grotesque, cruel feature of biology whereby we disconnect from reality, stop forming memories, stop moving, etc. and lay there for seven to ten hours every day recuperating from the previous day.

If this never struck you as strange, it's because you're not paying attention. As I mentioned in a previous post about time (and the lack of it), you will spend a 1/3 of your life horizontal in a non-optional hormonally regulated anabolic state required for neurological pruning, endocrine calibration, muscular repair, immune modulation - you name it - with no conscious effort required on your part, which is ultimately responsible for the maintenance of every biological function that sustains performance, health, and vitality.

Sleep is old—very old. What I mean by that is that sleep isn't some sort of recent evolutionary adaptation that only manifests in humans; it's a biological function shared by every creature in the animal kingdom. If you've spent your late-night entertainment watching a nature show, you'll probably completely overlook that every animal you see sleeps in some manner, even without a central nervous system (like jellyfish). Birds sleep mid-flight (wtf)- because it's necessary for survival - if it weren't, you wouldn't see it.

And have you ever thanked sleep? Of course not. In fact, you've probably spent most of your life resenting the interruption, joining others in saying nonsense like, 'I'll sleep when I'm dead,' generally doing your best to mimic the sleeping hygiene of a zombie. Well, after this article, perhaps you'll stop being so obtuse, straighten your life out, and stop sleeping like you're a nurse pulling an overtime shift.

Sleeping is Training

I propose a mindset shift: If you want to get the most out of your training and adapt maximally, you need to view sleep as a workout.

If this makes you want to edge away and run for the exit, I understand entirely. Realizing that sleep was so important to performance that I might as well view it as a workout itself was a very cold shower for me, as I'm sure it is for you.

Why?

Because you can't separate the efficacy of your training from the rest of your life. Unfortunately, you don't get to do your workout perfectly, sleep for five hours that night, and look yourself in the mirror and say, "I did everything I could." Well, you can, but you're just lying to yourself to make yourself feel better, which isn't something you should be proud of and would disappoint your mother.

Doing a perfect workout and then sleeping for five hours is like skipping a third of the intervals of the workout—you're spinning your wheels. What everyone forgets about spinning their wheels is that while it's happening, it feels like you're doing something; it takes a lot of effort, but no matter how far you stomp that gas pedal, the car (your fitness) doesn't move at all.

Sleep Hygiene

Much like wiping, showering, combing, and brushing, you can learn a known set of sleeping practices that will make you acceptable to yourself and society, collectively called 'sleep hygiene.' We can grossly complicate this, but instead of flooding this article with suggestions that sound like I cribbed from a horoscope, I'm going to tell you what to do by telling you what not to do:

  • Don't go to bed at a different time every night.

  • Don't go to bed so late that you have no chance to sleep 8+ hours.

  • Don't eat right before, or even within a few hours of, going to bed.

  • Don't drink alcohol and expect your sleep not to be shit.

  • Don't fall asleep while looking at a glowing blue screen filled with the most interesting, algorithmically determined content on the planet, unless you're a real man.

  • Don't turn the heat up in your room because someone told you you could get in your heat training while sleeping.

  • Don't fill your room with light so your brain thinks you live in some hell hole like Norway between June and August when the sun never goes down.

And if you don't do all that, what have you accomplished?

You've successfully slept optimally for one night! Congratulations - now do it again for the rest of your life spent trying to race bikes fast or for the period of time you care about your well-being and consistently showing up as your best self, not a brain-dead, hungover corpse, mostly wasting the oxygen they breathe.

But How Do I Know If It's Working?

Heart rate variability (HRV).

HRV is the natural variation in time between heartbeats that arises from the influence of the autonomic nervous system (ANS), the part of your nervous system you don't have control of.

The ANS has two branches, the sympathetic 'fight or flight' and the parasympathetic 'rest and digest.' which exerts a push and pull that manifests as the time between heartbeats. When the parasympathetic branch is strong, and recovery is going well (because you're not sleeping like an asshole!), HRV is high or variable. When the parasympathetic branch is weak, and recovery is shit (because you're sleeping like an asshole!), HRV is low or less variable.

I know—probably the last thing you want as an athlete is more numbers, but unfortunately, as much as I want it to be, HRV is not a made-up number that doesn't mean anything. HRV is an excellent indicator of an athlete's ability to recover, especially from high-intensity training (between aerobic and anaerobic thresholds) - the higher it is, the better the athlete responds. If you're serious about your training, you'd probably benefit by tracking HRV and trying to maximize it by not treating your body like a pinata.

And before any of you come at me with studies, sources, or personal anecdotes trying to debunk the validity of HRV as a powerful, helpful indicator of recovery and readiness to adapt to training stress, the exit is behind you. Don't let the door hit you.

I have used many different apps to measure and record HRV for over seven years at this point. I can say with confidence that even devices that are garbage at taking measurements and are generally frowned upon, like WHOOP, remain directionally correct and are far more helpful than the smug expressions of HRV truthers who will tell you to 'trust their vibes' while they add a couple of drops of Ivermectin to their morning coffee and tell you about all the great races they had back in 2014 when they were dunking on everyone at the local charity fun run.

Even if WHOOP is low-key shit, every time I woke up and saw a horrifying, basement-level HRV number outlined in red font, it wasn't picking up noise; it was picking up something valid. Like the ten beers I had the night before. Or that fight with my wife. Or general substance abuse for the last five years of my life I've somehow been able to sustain on five hours of sleep. Or the crippling effects of back-to-back VO2 max workouts followed by a recovery shake of nothing.
