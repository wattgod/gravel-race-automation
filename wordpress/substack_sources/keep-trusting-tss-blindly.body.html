Use Work, Not Stress

I don’t build periodize based on time, distance, or TSS.

Translation - instead of time, distance, or TSS, Iñaki uses work expressed in kilojoules to guide his training because it’s real, because time and distance are made up constructs, unlike the amount of energy required to apply a force of one Newton through a distance of one meter. Sorry, that sounded petty.

The big claim here is that the kjs expended during training tell you exactly what your body did. Except, it doesn’t.

Assuming we’re measuring kjs correctly (which most people in most sports can’t), citing the raw kilojoules expended only gives you a one dimensional view of the training - just like hours, or distance - that collapses the real goal of any metric that attempts to quantify training load - what was the physiological cost of the workout?

For example, you can expend 1000 kJ in 2 hours, or you can expend 1000 kjs in 1 hour, but the physiological cost of the former versus the latter is wildly different. The nutrient partitioning (the way energy is derived) required to produce 500 kjs every hour (moderate glycogen) is going to look a lot different than producing 1000 kJ every hour (A LOT of glycogen)

I’m going to skip a little bit ahead here and get to Inaki’s solution:

Don’t just train hard. Train inside what your body can recover from.

Use this:

Training Load = Weekly kJ × Intensity Distribution × Recovery Score

Where:

  • Weekly kJ = Total external load

  • Intensity = How much time in each zone

  • Recovery Score = Sleep, HRV, energy, stress

The problem with this is every one of those variables he relies on to calculate training load have caveats that undercut his claim. Let’s briefly examine each variables weaknesses:

  1. Weekly kjs - To calculate weekly kilojoules you need to calculate kilojoules expenditure from training activities which, we’ve already established (and he admitted) isn’t accurate.

  2. Intensity Distribution - Calculating Intensity distribution is a minefield because that relies on how -what testing methodology and protocol- an athlete/coach determines thresholds and zones and in a best case scenario is still an approximation of their ability which makes their training intensity distribution on any given day another approximation at best.

  3. Recovery Score - Calculating a recovery score will also necessarily rely on lots of sketchy measurements (are you measuring your HRV from a heart rate strap after waking up or from WHOOP?) and assumptions (How is the athlete reporting/quantifying stress?) that, again, provide an estimation at best.

So, if we rewrote the equation above, it would be something like Approximation x Approximation X Approximation = Superior training load metric. This may not surprise you, but I don’t find that method of training load calculation to be convincingly superior to using TSS.

Notice I said ‘convincingly superior’ there. I’m not making the claim that Iñaki’s training load calculation approach isn’t better, I just don’t think, both from a conceptual and ease of calculation standpoint that it’s as good as he’s making it out to be, and he makes that point for me when he says, “If your energy output is going up, your intensity is mostly in Zone 1, and your recovery is solid: you’re on the right track.” As you’ll see, the key clause here is “if your intensity is mostly in Zone 1.”

Arguably the end goal of all endurance training is to produce more output (speed or watts) with the less (or even the same) input (oxygen). Measuring training load in kilojoules will tell you if you’re on the right track IF, and only if you’re contextualizing kjs expenditure with the intensity at which it was burned, specifically if it’s being burned in a more or less completely aerobic (meaning with oxygen).

But you don’t need to calculate training load in kilojoules to see that, all an athlete needs to do is repeat the same steady state Z1 workout and record power or pace and see what their EF is.

In other words, kilojoules expenditure is only a meaningful training load metric until you add intensity to it which is the just answering the training load quantification problem that TSS tries to address from another angle without a clear advantage. So yeah, kilojoules are for losers, man (lol).

The real reason that measuring training load in kilojoules isn’t better than TSS isn’t because that’s not true on the merits, it’s because the reason athlete’s don’t train badly isn’t because the way they model training load is wrong, it’s because bad training is fun and smart training is boring.

That’s right - training like a moron is just funner (lol) than training intelligently. Consider:

What does bad fun training look like?

  • Going hard constantly.

  • Trying to drop everyone, even parapalegic and geriatric tandem riders to satisfy the ego.

  • Doing epic rides that well past your body’s ability to absorb or adapt to simply because your weekend is incomplete if someone’s ride on Strava was more epic than yours.

  • Underfueling on the bike to get painfully skinny even if it kills your power and recovery because looking fast is more important than being fast.

  • Mostly replenshing the enormous deficit generated from undereating by drinking booze and eating apps at trendy locations while pretending it’s a mystery why you look like a condom pulled over a skeleton.

  • Regularly bombing races and complaining to anyone who will listen on how much training you’ve been doing lately and it’s all part of your ‘plan'.

What does good boring training look like?

  • Carefully controlling training intensity.

  • Rarely trying to drop anyone outside a race, and even then only doing so when it makes the most tactical sense, never to make a statement for the ego.

  • Doing the least amount of training for the maximum adaptation, which results in most Strava ride posts as boring.

  • Fueling correctly before, during, and after training to maximize execution and adaptation.

  • Not engaging in the Licensing Effect whereby someone gives themselves mental permission to drink, overeat, and generally treat their body like a trash panda just bc they rode for awhile.

  • Understand that racing is not an FTP test, but it’s own skillset and regularly gets in the arena to sharpen tactical, logistical, nutritional, psychological acumen which they’ll need just as much as they do a big engine when the gun goes off.

The Real Issue

Inaki explicitly mentions who the inspiration for measuring training load by kilojoules is in his post - Alan Couzens - a somewhat famous triathlon coach that’s been ahead of the mainstream training consensus for years because he’s methodical, able to code (which allows him to use data from athletes he’s worked with to explore questions most coaches can’t begin to answer but simply blindly parrot other’s perspectives), and opinionated, to put it mildly.

Or, as Inaki put it:

And if there’s one person who’s been doing this long before it was trendy, it’s Alan Couzens. I’ve seen it. Years ago, before the endurance world knew who he was, I called himThe MAD Scientist, not because he was crazy, but because his ideas were too far ahead for most people to accept.

Later, we became a small group, what he called The MAD Crew. Elite athletes, age groupers, and a few coaches who trusted his “crazy” thinking. Now we all gathered in a private forum to share data, progress, and ideas that felt years ahead of the mainstream: MAD Crew Forum

And what’s the problem with Alan’s science?

Nothing - he’s brilliant, honestly. I mean, the guy wrote a goddamn textbook called the Science of Maximal Athletic Development which should be required reading for anyone that’s even looked at a bike.

In it, he convincingly demonstrates what it takes to fully develop as an endurance athlete and builds his case by demonstrating that most of modern life undermines that pursuit and therefore a key pillar of becoming as fast as you could be on a bike (or running, or swimming) requires changing your life priorities, commitments, and circumstances to facilitate maximum athletic development, which is true and also exactly where he loses most people.

Why?

People don’t want to maximally develop athletically; they want to maximize fun.

Athletes don’t ignore his ideas, or debate whether to measure their training load by hours, or TSS, or kilojoules any more than they consider quitting their day job and not having kids so they can move to Flagstaff, AZ and refocus the next twenty years of their life on putting out a few more watts per heart beat. Those conversations are completely pointless until you can convince someone to care more about their long term performance rather than their short term fun.

Metrics don’t need to change, what an athlete is trying to get out of it does.
