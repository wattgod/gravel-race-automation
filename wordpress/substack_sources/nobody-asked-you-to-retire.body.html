It’s almost been a year since I stopped working at TrainingPeaks. When I left, I was one of the longest-serving employees in the company, departing just three months short of my twelve-year Workversary (lol).

My departure was one of the most anticlimactic, cold experiences of my life. I didn’t get an exit interview. No one put together a happy hour. I wasn’t asked to send a company-wide goodbye email. No one said, “It was nice working with you, thanks for all you’ve done for the company for over a decade.”

Instead, an HR lady I’d never met in person, who hadn’t even been there a quarter of my tenure, instantly replied to the news of my departure with a canned list of things I had to do before I left, and that was that.

That’s right—after all that time with TrainingPeaks, my last contact with the company was an impersonal automated email reply, probably composed by AI, which didn’t so much as wish me well on whatever my forthcoming projects were, but seemed really fixated on making sure I dropped off my work computer at my nearest FedEx drop box as soon as possible and signed and understood my NDA.

This hurt my feelings, or it would have if the corporate HR slop and theatrical indifference weren’t so blatant that it almost came off as so performative I had to laugh. When reading the HR lady’s offboarding emails, you could feel the joy she felt in each of her frigid, vaguely threatening bullet points. I have felt more warmth from a robot waiter at a sushi restaurant.

Yet, I wasn’t mad at her. On the contrary, I was strangely relieved, because it confirmed a suspicion I’ve had for years about the underlying reality of employment: your job doesn’t care about you. Especially when your employer is a wannabe corporation like TrainingPeaks.

Of course, companies deny this because they don’t want to come off as the unrepentant capitalistic psychopaths that they are and say creepy shit like “Everyone at this company is a family” (free tip: if you ever hear that from your employer, or a place you’re thinking of applying to, run!).

But the truth is that every employee is a line item on a spreadsheet, and if your inclusion in the profit and loss statement hurts the company’s math, they will kick you to the curb without blinking an eye and sugarcoat that reality with statements like “We’re going in a different direction because of economic headwinds.”

Meanwhile, the owner flies back and forth on his private jet once a quarter to give long, folksy speeches about how everyone needs to work harder and how everyone is already paid well enough at the company, while blowing half a million dollars on leadership who say things like “We’re leveraging cross-functional synergies to align key stakeholders, unlock scalable growth opportunities, and produce meaningful impact across the broader ecosystem” with a straight face, but release nothing and make the company meaningfully worse.

But this chilly, willful apathy at my departure confirmed that hunch and spared me from the usual platitudinous gaslighting companies normally engage in when someone leaves.

At least they were honest in the end.

A Parallel Timeline

Instead of TrainingPeaks kicking me to the curb without so much as a goodbye, imagine management cared about me and I cared about them.

Imagine that I, a moderately successful glorified customer service rep, announced that, after almost 12 years of waking up every day eager to optimize the TrainingPeaks athlete and coach customer experience, I’d decided to step away from the company to pursue another opportunity.

In a tearful, 1,400-word company memo (which I reposted on LinkedIn), I thank my managers, my colleagues, and above all my family and children for putting up with me staying up late for all those Dreaming Seasons so I could fulfill my dreams of maximizing shareholder value while I volunteered to take pay cuts to help pay for the salaries of senior leadership who took half of their conference calls from their horse barn with their camera turned off.

But I hadn’t given my two weeks, mind you. I’d given my fifty-two weeks.

I wasn’t leaving soon. Before I hung up my lanyard and returned the IKEA furniture they gave me when the pandemic hit, I was going to spend the next four quarters saying goodbye to all the customers I’d helped, attend my last few TrainingPeaks Universities so I could thank all our dedicated coaches for the opportunity to share our life-changing software, before contributing a few more killer assets to top-priority go-to-market strategies in our annual Dreaming Season push.

All while not just working from the office, but living from it so management knew how dedicated I was to the TrainingPeaks mission.

Maybe I’d commission a short documentary about my final product launch. Maybe a limited run of commemorative quarter-zips. Perhaps a farewell webinar where longtime customers could join me one last time to discuss the Annual Training Plan.

After all, this company had given me so much.

You Think That Sounds Crazy? This Is How Amateur Athletes “Retire.”

If you thought that sounded unhinged, perhaps you’ll begin to understand how I feel when I see people announce their retirement from cycling (or other athletic pursuits), especially when the retiree in question wasn’t even earning a living from it, but was an amateur who simply decided to stop pursuing their hobby competitively.

The formula for this is remarkably consistent:

  1. A (supposedly) candid, solemn iPhone portrait of the athlete from a distance, their face fixed contemplatively on the horizon.

  2. A long Instagram post that includes stock expressions such as “After much reflection…” and “one of the hardest decisions I’ve ever made,” and thanks to “all the people who pushed me to become the person I am today,” among a sometimes Russian-novel-level inclusion of setbacks and personal takeaways.

  3. A declaration that they’re no longer competing, but the sport will always be a part of who they are.

  4. That even though they’re no longer doing the thing that made people follow them in the first place, this is “just the beginning” and to “stay tuned for what’s next,” as if people will still care in the same way if they stop doing the thing that made them followable.

  5. All of this is set to extremely melancholic acoustic covers of songs that are normally fine but feel like emotional waterboarding when overlaid on the post.

Don’t believe me? Go back through the timelines of your friends who retired and tell me how many of those boxes they checked. I’m sure any posts you find will have at least three out of five, and you might even uncover similarities I didn’t notice.

The strange thing about these announcements is that nobody is questioning the person’s right to stop competing. Nobody is demanding that they continue. Nobody is even necessarily aware that they were still competing in the first place.

They’ve decided to stop paying money to enter races, and somehow this requires a public accounting of their career.

Imagine announcing your retirement from restaurants because you’ve decided to stop eating at Applebee’s.

And yet, in sports, we treat this as perfectly normal.

The Retirement GOAT

But before you dig through your friends’ timelines, you have to consider the all-time most self-indulgent retirement announcement, which is, surprisingly, not Phil Gaimon, the former WorldTour climber who has spent more time making money as a retired cyclist than as an employed one, but Peter Stetina.

Who is Peter Stetina?

A former WorldTour road cyclist turned gravel privateer who, in December 2025, announced a farewell tour alongside a documentary about his failed attempt to set a record time on a hill climb. The tour included Mid South, Belgian Waffle Ride California, Oregon Trail, Steamboat, and Levi’s GranFondo.

He also planned to continue with Canyon in a mentoring role.

But, curiously, Stetina wasn’t saying he’d stop racing, just that he was retiring from chasing professional podiums.

That’s right—homie wasn’t quitting cycling. He just wasn’t trying to win anymore, and wanted the world to know he wasn’t trying hard anymore, in case the world somehow confused his increasingly slow cycling results with his talent rather than his intent.

Think about that for a while.

A farewell tour.

For himself.

Stetina had a legitimate WorldTour career. He did some impressive gravel shit. Fine. But why does that make organizing a yearlong celebration of yourself any less self-indulgent?

What other career or profession affords such incredible self-indulgence?

Imagine if I announced that I was no longer pursuing high-level performance in the customer education space, but would be making a series of farewell appearances at my favorite TrainingPeaks Universities before transitioning into an advisory role.

I’d be institutionalized.

And the amateur version is even more absurd. At least Stetina actually made a living racing bicycles. Somewhere out there is a 43-year-old Cat 3 with two career podiums, one in a field of seven, preparing to announce the end of his competitive journey.

He has sacrificed. His family has sacrificed. His employer has sacrificed approximately 45 minutes of productivity every Monday morning while he uploads his race file to Strava.

And now he’s stepping away.

From what, precisely?

Nobody employed him to race bicycles. Nobody depended on his continued participation. No contract to terminate, no roster spot to vacate, no younger riders anxiously awaiting his departure.

He paid money to enter races.

And now he won’t.

Congratulations on your retirement from being a customer.

We Have Decided to Step Away

There’s another specific habit in cycling that I think belongs to the same phenomenon: the use of the royal “we.”

Lance Armstrong was particularly fond of speaking in the plural. “We” trained harder. “We” had the best equipment. “We” executed the plan.

Now, to be fair, professional road cycling really is a team sport, and Armstrong had an entire organization of riders, mechanics, directors, soigneurs, and other staff supporting him. In that context, “we” makes perfect sense.

But somewhere along the way, this manner of speaking trickled down into amateur cycling, where a man with a power meter, a TrainingPeaks subscription, and a coach he pays $250 a month starts talking like he’s the CEO of a publicly traded company.

“We’re happy with where the fitness is.”

“We’ve decided to focus on gravel this season.”

“We’re going to review our priorities after Nationals.”

Who the fuck is we?

You and your legs?

Your wife, who found out about your race schedule after you’d already registered?

Your coach, who is currently working with seventeen other people and probably doesn’t know what you do for a living?

It’s an incredible linguistic trick. A person who rides a bicycle has somehow become an organization. His training is now a collective enterprise. His race calendar serves as a strategic initiative. The entire organization needs to address his inability to hold 400 watts for five minutes.

And, naturally, when the time comes to retire, “we” have decided it’s time to step away.

I suspect this all stems from the same desire to make our athletic pursuits feel more consequential than they actually are.

We train like professionals. We buy the equipment of professionals. We have coaches, nutrition strategies, training camps, recovery methods, sponsors, race calendars, and carefully managed social media presences.

Eventually, after years of pretending our amateur athletic pursuits are professional careers, we decide we ought to retire like professionals, too.

It’s professional-athlete cosplay taken to its logical conclusion.
