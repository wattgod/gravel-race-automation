September 2, 2018 - GMSR - Queen Stage, "App Gap"

“KOM leader, Matti Rowe, please come up!”
(Caught off guard, I hunch down).


Matti Rowe, please come forward!”
(I hunch down further).


“Alright, I guess we’ll just start!”

I didn’t realize that I had actually won the KOM competition the prior day and should be wearing the KOM leader’s jersey. The Technical Guide says this explicitly.

The race started 45 minutes late, 45 minutes where I sat on a brick wall paying absolutely no attention to my bowel status.

We began from the parking lot of a downhill ski area, and the race was neutral down the steep 9% incline. 52 racers pushed off, and all you could hear is the metallic hum of the packs' freewheels.

Unlike seemingly everyone around me, I was nervous because this is exactly the sort of ‘nothing’ moment when something catastrophic happens. And of course, it did.

A rider in front of me rode his front wheel into one of Vermont's innumerable frost-heaved asphalt cracks and went down. Instinct saved me, and I swerved, missing him by a few feet.

What happened to that guy? He DNFed (did not finish)—done. He is out of the race. All those months of training, the money, and the emotional investment were undone because he let his attention slip for one second. Bike racing is savage.

Don’t thwow it!

Hadley, right before she throws every item of breakfast food in front of her on the floor.

Eventually, we all made it down to the country road, the whistle blew, and we were racing. Three miles down, only 100 more to go, and not just any 100 miles, 100 miles with 9,000ft of climbing that ended on a freaking 15% grade for 500 meters. Let’s do this!

Unfortunately, just as I thought I had noticed another sensation beyond my stoke level—a burning sensation downstairs—I had to go. Thankfully, #1, but still, I really had to go.

For a few miles, I contemplated my options. Could I hold it for the next 4.5 hours? I let myself buy into this fantasy for a few minutes, but realized I couldn’t think about anything else, and there was no way in hell I could race effectively with my urinary tract preparing a coup attempt.

So, how do I relieve myself? Some bike racers can go off their bike while they’re moving. I’ve never been one of those people. Call it stage fright. Call it modesty. Call it an inability to relax my bowels while riding a bike at 25 mph in a pack. Whatever the case, it wasn’t happening.

I found the Cat 2 KOM GMSR Jersey on sale on Ebay please help money so I can finally reclaim what’s mine.


That left stopping. My heart sank. This is the best way to relieve the urge, but it is often perilous because you have to actually stop. Stopping, even if the peloton isn’t going fast, is always stressful, because even when they’re not going fast, they’re going a whole lot faster than not moving at all, so that means you’ll have to chase them down.

I went for it, drifting back in the pack, then stopped hurriedly and began to empty. Thirty seconds passed, 45 seconds passed, a minute passed, and I didn’t feel even close to done. I counted 100 seconds. The peloton was out of view, the caravan that followed it was gone, and every race official except for one motorcycle race official, who was posted up a polite 30 feet away idling, was gone.

I glanced over at him. He shook his head slowly back and forth and pointed at his watch as if to say ‘Dude!! Are you going for a pissing record?? Let’s go!”

I flipped my boys back in, saddled up, and put off in haste after the race. And of course, I was quickly met by a shallow, 1-2% sustained downhill, which is the worst thing that I could have encountered. A pack can go down that much faster than a rider alone because once the front rider reaches peak speed, those behind can slingshot around and in that way an entire group can move 5+mph faster.

I wasn’t exactly panicking, but I knew that in all likelihood, a long, solitary chase awaited me, and that means using a lot of energy, the energy I should be saving for the end of the race when it mattered.

Just when I set my jaw, the Moto official drove up next to me, pulled in front of me, and looked back to see that I was in the draft. Astonished, I hopped on. He increased the speed. I looked at my Garmin, and our speed grew from 30 mph to 35 mph, then to 40mph, 45 mph, then 50mph! We were drilling it down the canyon. I was giving it everything I could to stay in the motorcycle's draft, and the driver was basically idling. I felt laughably weak.

The Caravan gradually pulled into view, then the peloton. Right before we got close, he swung off, drew up beside me, motioned with his finger ‘Shhhhh’, and then dropped back. Wow.

To put this in context, in 6 years of racing, I have NEVER known an official to help a racer like that - not once. If anything, they sit behind you silently, bored, and watch you die a thousand deaths just to get back to the peloton. No kindness, no mercy, but not in Vermont, I guess?

By the time I caught back on, we were only about 25 miles into the 103-mile race. That’s a long way to go. What should I do? Attack? Sit at the back and save energy?

The answer is neither. What you do is something called ‘Active Patience’. I just made that up, and I find the name kind of boring, so let’s call it FBRS (freaking boring race strategy). You use just enough energy to stay near the front, but you do not attack until the last hour of the race.

That sounds simple, I know, but in practice, it’s another thing. Remember that everyone has been training for this race for months, waiting for this day, the Queen stage, where the race will be won or lost.

Everyone feels strong and wants to drop everyone else. But you know what? No one is stronger than 50 other people. If they were, they wouldn’t be racing in this field. So, you have to pick your moments, and you know when the moment almost never is? In the first 3 hours of the race.

So you wait. You eat expensive, sugary foods. You try to pay attention so you don’t crash. You try not to do too much. But, at some point, you have to start playing your hand, because you never really know what breakaway is dangerous or not.

That moment was 80 miles into the race. The Yellow Jersey (the current overall leader) attacked the peloton because a dangerous group of 10 people was up the road. Their gap grew. I knew I couldn’t let them go and still finish where I need to finish.

I went to the front to pull, and no one else would help. After I got done pulling, they simply stayed in my draft and we lost our momentum. Fed up, I attacked. Suddenly, everyone had energy and could follow me! I went back to pulling, and no one would help. I attacked again, and the same thing happened - they jumped on my attack but would not pull.

One guy in particular, a guy from NYC with a nose ring and tattoos, seemed eager to jump on my every move, but would not pull. He did not have a teammate up the road. There was no reason to do what he was doing, except that he was resigned to not making it to the group in front and wanted as many people to stay here with him, sucking.

Enraged, I waited for a short uphill. I turned around, gave the NYC guy the middle finger, and attacked up the hill. I know - not my classiest moment. None of them could follow. I made it to the group up the road. I still had a chance at a result.

Some moments really, really matter. You do one thing, and an entire ocean of new possibilities opens up. If you don’t, you’re right back in the nowhere you’ve always been. No one likes to hear this, but you only get so many chances.

When I attacked the peloton and bridged to the leaders, I attacked with 5 years of failure and self-loathing. I was done with the cheap optimism of potential, in pushing what I wanted out to a future outside my control.

I grabbed hold of my hatred and left my old life behind with every foot I put between myself and the peloton.

I remember the first time I did an endurance workout. I was at my grandparent's farm in Edgar, Wisconsin, 17 years ago. I had nothing to do. For some wild reason, I decided that meant I should run 6 miles. I don’t know where I got that number, but I do know I had never run anywhere near that far before.

I could have watched TV. I could have read. I could have done anything else, but I felt, with strange certainty, that I needed to run, and run far, beyond what I knew.

I ran for 6 miles on the gravelly shoulder of Highway H, a hilly county road to nowhere. In the end, I could barely walk. I remember laboring up the hills, the ragged breathing, the fatigue taking over. I remember my Grandmother’s astonishment when I returned. I remember feeling weak and slow. I remember happiness in the afterglow of my own destruction.

I approached the final 500 meters of the 103-mile Green Mountain Stage Race Appalachian Gap road race in fifth place. I was annihilated, but I knew if I could just make it up this 15% grade, I would get 5th. I would get the points to get to Cat 1.

It was too steep to sit on the bike and pedal, so I got out of the saddle and closed my eyes, leaning into the pain. Memories flashed across my mind: the sweet ache of that run long ago; my freshman year of Nordic skiing in college, my teammates laughing at me because I was so slow; all those hours alone riding in the cold that came to nothing; the dismissive look of strangers when they asked me if I was a Cat 1, and I told them I was a 2; and the sneers of everyone who told me I was too fat to ever race bikes fast.

I crossed the finish in 5th. The first thing I saw was my wife and daughter. Over the squeals of ‘Dada! Dada!’ I knew I had done it. I was hollowed out and broken, just like when I made it back to the driveway of the farm 17 years ago in Wisconsin. Everything felt lifted and far away, but my breath was close.

I sucked in the cold, clean air of Vermont. With every exhale, my old story of failure broke apart and fell away. Standing at the finish, I felt joined with a moment on a park bench in Oslo seven years ago when I knew I would win Alt for Norge and everything would change. Then, as now, I stared into the sky and said goodbye to a few demons for the last time.

I never became what I dreamed, but I did become what I am. There’s a victory in that.

September 3rd, 2018 - Burlington Criterium Race

Of course, I wasn’t done yet; I had a Criterium to finish. 7th place overall was a minute behind me, so I’d have to have a catastrophic race to cede that much time. I knew that wasn’t beyond me.

Usually, stage race criteriums are easy. Not Green Mountain Stage race. The course traveled up a sharp hill, around a corner up a brick false flat, snaked around a few corners, and slingshotted you out through a brakeless downhill into the finish. Oh, and the entire damn thing was littered with potholes and man covers just begging to crash a rider out.

For most of the week, temperatures hovered in the 60s. Today, Burlington boiled over, the sun lashing the streets in 95-degree heat. With 10 laps to go, I was cross-eyed, had burned through both water bottles, and was begging for the line. The pace increased, but not beyond my will, which was boosted every lap with whoops from Betsy, who faithfully cheered me with Hadley on a crucial part of the course.

I crossed the line, sealing 6th overall. 5/52 in the road race, 6/53 overall - 15 points. I had it, probably. I would be Cat 1, maybe.

Some moments in life, fleeting as they are, are especially sweet. You’ve worked alone, quietly, for a long time, years maybe, and you finally get there. You were never guaranteed this; your path was fraught with peril, but suddenly, you’ve made it. You’re living in it, looking out into a different world.

That’s where I found myself as my family and I drifted along the streets of Burlington. We settled on a French restaurant next to the racecourse on the downtown streets. We ordered expensive cheeses and wine, Hadley tried to order a chicken all on her own, and we made a great mess. We watched the professional racers go by and spent too much. It was well spent.

Long ago, physicists couldn't account for the way particles interacted, so they invented a word for that undetectable medium: ether. They were completely wrong; ether doesn’t exist, but the word persisted colloquially as ‘something in the ether.’

That phrase kept swimming through my head as we finished our meal and walked through the sun-baked streets together. I felt as though I was swimming through ether, which is to say I felt that I had finally arrived, finally redeemed myself on the shores of Lake Champlain.

When you’ve waited so long in vain, the sensation of fruition isn’t a default. You feel it in starts. Something from your depths rises up, maybe doubt, maybe failure, and you reach instinctively for your old answers, but find that you have an entirely new response to everything within that says you can’t.

Hads has her limits, and we listened, so we headed back from Burlington to our condo in Warren. I kept the windows down, letting the sweet inflow of early September air rush through the van. Maybe it won’t be the last time I’m here, but I treated it like it was, as you always should.

If no one shares this, I will refuse to release the last part of this series out of spite.
