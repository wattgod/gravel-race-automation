September 4th, 2018 - Returning Home

Our flight left ungodly early, so we awoke at 3:30 am, packed our shit, and got to the airport. Our 3rd Stage Race (every connecting flight with Hadley is the equivalent mental and physical energy of a 4-day stage race) in 5 days had begun.

Hadley took her pound of flesh from us both. We deserved it. The connection from Chicago to Denver marks the 37th flight of her life, and now that she’s surpassed the physical autonomy of an inert meat sack, I think we’ll be putting future flights on pause until an iPad can entirely consume the angst of flight.

September 5th, 2018 - Upgrade Application

I wasted no time in applying for my upgrade. I just wanted to know. I knew if I didn’t get it, I still had the Tour de Los Alamos in my back pocket, but I just wanted to be done.

I know all my talk about ether and transcendence had you thinking the upgrade was a certainty, but I knew it actually was going to be dicey; I just didn’t bother disclosing that so Betsy would actually support me going to Vermont.

So, in 2013, the governing body, USA Cycling, made a rule change saying that upgrade points never expired. I was overjoyed. That means that you didn’t have to get all the upgrade points you needed in one season; you could get some every couple of seasons and gradually earn the 35 you needed to get to Cat 1. It was doable.

In 2017, that all changed.

You read that right—36 months. When did I score my first upgrade points in the P12 category? 2013-14. 36/12 = 3 years. It’s 2018, so only results from 2015 onward count. How many points did I score then? 4. So whatever I had to have 35 points from 2015 onward. Shit.

That brings us to the next point. So as far as upgrade points go, Stage races are a goddamn gold mine. Check out this chart.

Did you notice anything? The field size is a MASSIVE factor in how many points are at stake. If it’s 50+ people, the winner gets 20 freaking points! Twenty points is over half the points you need to get from Cat 2 to Cat 1.

But wait, what if there are only 49 people to start? The points drop precipitously. If you win, you drop down from 20 to 10. Ten points is the equivalent of winning a road race with more than 50 P12 people in it (read - freaking hard). Moreover, the placing that pays out drastically shrinks as well. More than 50 people in the stage race, and you get 15th overall? You get a point. 49 people? You only get one point if you’re in 8th place. If there was one more person, bringing the field to 50, 8 points. In other words, field size matters big time.

53 people started the Green Mountain Cat 2 Stage Race in 2018. That means that, in theory, all the available upgrade points were on the board. I say in theory, because while the fine print says ‘starters’, I’ve heard of people getting dinged because the upgrade bureaucracy did not judge your field size by the number of starters, but by the number of finishers.

Are the implications not sinking in? Remember that guy who crashed right in front of me on the neutral section of the 3rd stage and took me out? Yeah, 52 guys started that race. That means that off the bat, only 51 people could finish. That doesn’t leave a lot of wiggle room for the field size to retain the maximum number of points for the race. If 2 people didn’t finish the hardest single day of road racing in New England, the field size would drop to 49, which puts you in the lower points table.

Sure enough, only 48 people finished that race, even though 52 people started. I understand you might be lost or bored, but the key question is this: Would the administrator of USA Cycling count the number of starters or the number of finishers in their calculation of points? If it was the latter, I left Vermont with 15 points: 5 points for finishing 5/52 starters on the queen stage and 6th/53 starters in the overall stage race. If it were total finishers, I had 6 points total, 9 points less. In other words, I wouldn’t have close to the number of points I needed.

I wish I could say this is where the technicality-laced upgrade anxiety ended. Unfortunately, there was the small matter of a race in New Mexico in which I had scored points that was in some dispute. Check out this disgraceful results sheet.

Notice anything? Yeah - ALL THE FREAKING CATEGORIES ARE JUMBLED TOGETHER.
Let me preface all this by saying that I love New Mexico, its roads, and its races. It hosts my favorite race of all time, the Tour of the Gila, after all.

That said, the organization there is a shit show. The Bill McClain Sandia Road Race wasn’t advertised until 1 week before the race started. I wouldn’t even have known about it unless I was looking for it, and even then, it was hard to register. It was a killer race, but freaking haphazard. After, I contacted the organizer to get a clean bill of results separated by category results so I could submit for the upgrade. Did I get a response? You bet your ass I didn’t.

Further complicating things, even though the race took 3.5 hours and finished on an hour-long climb, it doesn’t meet USA Cycling’s minimum road race criteria for a road race length, 85 miles, so the points in it are less than they should be.

And here is the irony. On the site, with the results jumbled, USA Cycling awarded me six upgrade points for that result. They’re wrong, at most I should have received 3, at least, 1.

In any case, my total points submitted through my resume, including my 2013-2014 result, which shouldn’t count because it was too old, was 40. So, less than the result, I had 36, or one more than required for an upgrade. But that’s including the Sandia Crest Result, so probably, I had 34, or 1 less than I needed—one freaking point short.

So when I submitted, it was a stretch. I submitted on September 5th, 2018, 5.5 years after I submitted my upgrade from Category 3 to Category 2. This is a great summary of those first few days after the submission:

It was agony. What the hell was Stephanie doing? Probably drinking a Pumpkin Spice latte down in Colorado Springs, ignoring her inbox on purpose, knowing that her indecision was leaving me in existential knots.

Then, on September 7th, 2018, after enough refreshing to crash any browser, I saw this:

I didn’t know what that meant, so I went to the main page of my USA Cycling account.

I immediately texted my Wife.

I was approved. I am a fucking Category 1 road cyclist!! I was at work, and I immediately grabbed a bottle of champagne that TrainingPeaks received after Team Sky won the Tour de France, and considered opening it up and spraying it all over everyone's computer.

I didn’t, because I didn’t want to destroy seven work computers and get fired. I loudly announced around the office that I had finally crossed an arbitrary line and would now rage quit and vigorously pursue a dad-bod.

I sent off the announcement to anyone who would listen.

September 8th, 2018 - I can’t freaking believe it

I made it to Cat 1. Someone once told me that there wasn't a point in getting to Cat 1, you could do almost all of the same races as a Cat 2, and that it was just something you could tell someone in a bar. They're wrong. Labels matter. They don't give the whole story, but the world doesn't have the time to hear all your explanations.

Some of you are also snickering because it's not as if I became a doctor, or maybe you accomplished this when you were 18, and it's pathetic that I'm celebrating this when I'm 31 and just barely reaching the top level of elite amateur cycling. What a vainglorious no-talent ass-hat!

You're right. But modest as this achievement is, I'm proud of it, and you can stick it. I know countless friends and acquaintances who soared through Category 5, 4, and 3, only to ruin themselves against the brutal wall of P12 and quit. I didn't. I fell in love with it instead and pushed on. I know some of you really loved my failures. Good. I'm smiling also. They never defined me.


Epilogue

I haven’t ridden my bike since, I’ve gained 25 pounds, and I’ll never touch my bike again. I can finally rest and merge with my couch.

If only that were true. The journey has changed me. I made it to Cat 1 because of my choices, and those choices were changes in habits that became changes in me, which became indistinguishable from who I am.

I’m honest. I love discomfort. I love the mornings, it's bite, it’s challenge, fresh air pouring over my body, and the sun threatening to rise over the distant curve of the earth. I love my heart pumping in my chest, and that elusive feeling of transcendence going up a climb faster than my genes intended me to.. I like watching what I eat. I like long, hard days, when you’re reduced to what you are, suffering there, and going on despite it, escaping, just for a moment, mortality.

I owe cycling everything that’s saved me. It has redeemed me in my own shortcomings, led me down roads I never would have traveled, and tasted ecstasies I never would have known.

I don’t know if there’s more of a truth than the road, the simple places it goes. I will say, especially, there are the mountains, there is thin air, and steep grades, it’s an unceasing challenge, and silence. I’m tempted to say that is my truth, that who I am is someone alone on a bike in the morning on a far-flung road, and that’s enough.

I know I’ve arrived at what I was always coming to. It took longer than it should, and for that, I am ever grateful.
