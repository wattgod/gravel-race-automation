My 'Don't quit your day job' moment

Look, one thing I've discovered during my humble career and have done a horrible job of relating, partially because I'm embarrassed, is that I'm washed (peaked, over the hill, etc) and have been since 2021.

What happened in 2021? I raced Redlands Cycling Classic, arguably the most challenging stage race in North America (especially now that almost every other race has tanked).

What was it like? A pussy's nightmare.

Redlands is harder than anything I've ever done, not because of the race but because of the racers. Everyone at Redlands is at Redlands because Redlands is Redlands, and they want to dunk on everyone else and catch the right person's eye, the sort of person who knows a guy that could get you on a team where they hand out things called 'contracts' which give you something very rare in cycling - money (usually less than working as a waiter at Subway though, lol).

What does a race look like with 150+ racers with that mindset? Madness. Absolute fucking madness.

The moment the gun went off on the first stage of Redlands, those at the front exploded like it was a finishing sprint, and the blob of mostly delusional tryhards re-formed into a painful conga line that stretched several blocks. For any of you out there that suck at bikes as I do and have raced a bit, you'll notice a cruel irony in bike racing where yes, the front of the race is hard, but still rarely as hard as the back of the race filled with poor bastards not strong enough to be at the front but suffering harder because the stop, start nature in a pack, when rippling over the distance of a block and a half, turns a brief brake tap at the front into an RV sized length at the back for the pack fodder to cross lest they get dropped. This phenomenon is called the concertina effect.

Given my immense positional gifts, I found myself at the very back of this conga line, trying to bridge the very worst gaps that formed almost 200 racers with limited places to maneuver. The race was a circuit through a suburban neighborhood punctuated by one painful 90-second long, 8% hill we did over 30 times. Once you crested the hill, there was a false flat into a screaming downhill where we hit 48 mph before violently banking right into the entrance of a neighborhood where we snaked improbably around roundabouts without braking before finally emerging onto a wider street where everyone fanned out and tried to pass before sharply turning right again and sprinting back up the hell hill.

Where was I in all this? Riding in the butthole of the peloton, puckered but seething, lacking the technical ability, aggression, and, most of all, the balls to chop, elbow, and yell my way out past all the idiots around me so I wouldn't get dropped early and spend the rest of my precious vacation away from my family sitting in a follow car watching my teammates' bike race. In the end, I got pulled by race officials with less than ten laps to go, which wasn't bad enough to get me kicked out of the race but also not good enough to feel any pangs of pride.

If I did feel pride, it was because, ironically, the cowardice that kept me desperately clawing at the last few spots in the peloton had kept me out of three to five crashes that happened with other races fast and daring enough to fight for a good position.

Oh yeah - crashes. Did I mention the crashes at Redlands are absolutely bonkers? Every stage at Redlands (barring the time trial) has at least 2-3 crashes that range from 'not a big deal' to 'career-ending' (thankfully, there hasn't been a life-ending one yet 🤞). Why? That's just what happens when hundreds of ambitious, fast, aggressive people on bikes get funneled into narrow spaces at speed - bars overlap, attention momentarily slips, wheels touch, and suddenly two to fifty people hit the deck and disconnect their collarbones and destroy the equipment they often had to go into debt to buy.

Zwift Holes

In the age of power meters, data, and virtual platforms, we've entered an era in cycling in which:

  1. Race fitness demands are known.

  2. They're easy to measure.

  3. Many people can do it.

Now, the random freak living in, say, Rosendale, Wisconsin, won't be passed over or forgotten because even though they live far from established cycling centers, with a power meter, computer, smart indoor trainer, and an internet connection, it doesn't matter. The only question that matters, especially when you don't have to steer, is 'how many watts you got on you.'

What every moron who ever bought a power meter and put together a halfway decent time on a climb doesn't understand about bike racing is that bike racing is racing - and racing IS NOT getting skinny and pedaling really hard up a hill. Racing requires (relevant qualities in bold):

  1. Arrogance

  2. Ruthlessness

  3. Conserving energy

  4. Great positioning

  5. Smart tactics

  6. Patience

  7. Aggression

  8. Willingness to suffer

  9. Risk-taking

  10. Nutritional execution

  11. Pace changes

  12. Logistical execution

  13. Etc.

So, as much as the bolded items don't look like an equation, if you add together, arrogance +ruthlessness+ great Positioning + aggression + risk-taking, that equals an easily identifiable type of person in one word: a dick.

Yes, good bike racers are dicks (at least when they race!). They're ruthless, arrogant people who position themselves well in the bunch and aggressively take risks to win. They're not there to make friends, they're not there for the scenery, they're not kind people, they're there to win, and everything we do to pretend otherwise is cope.

Nature vs Nurture

I was talking to an athlete the other day, and they described some sort of warm, highly social event that's inclusive of all riders of all abilities and all ages, unites the community, and uplifts everyone through sport, in contrast to evil events like road races. I worked very hard not to laugh.

People say road cyclists are arrogant, and I agree with you, but what few people appreciate is the collective honesty of that detestable species of bike rider. They might be dicks, but they're also pretty open about it. Their shallowness and contempt for those who dress wrong or don't pedal as hard are quite naked.

Contrast that with other disciplines, like gravel events, that go to enormous lengths to provide multiple distances, great aid stations, zany courses, incentive non-lycra outfits, and furnish entrants with food trucks and beer credits and desperate local bands all to obscure the fact that people hoovered over several hundred dollars with the hopes of crushing their rivals but have to settle for an embarrassing mid-pack finish given all the time you trained for it. Now, they won't talk to their family, who could have gone to the waterpark but decided to show up to support them instead, ruining their Saturday. What the hell happened to gravel?

Mountain biking used to be cool, but then people ruined it. Road cycling used to be cool, but then people ruined it. Cyclocross used to be cool, but then people ruined it. Gravel was so cool it even had a spirit, but the spirit was dead even before it was widely known to be a thing.

I think when observers lament the demise of a cycling discipline that used to be so pure and clean, they're joining billions of other humans who see the same echoes in every other facet of human life in which a utopian beginning was inexplicably squandered by the idiocy of people, and if only we could get a fresh start, this time, this time we could get it right. These same people, these hectoring haul monitors masquerading as cycling influencers bitch in the media how gravel 'used to be cool' but how the scene had changed as it got more popular and if people would 'just be cool' the spirit of gravel, the beloved racing utopia we were promised, would return.

Just be cool? That's a hilarious thing to say because that phrase is at the bottom of so many people's laments about what 'other' people do. I mean, really, why can't the thieves, rapists, pedophiles, murderers, and various other flavors of human criminality 'just be cool'?

What's a Girl to Do?

Don't be jailbait. Don't get caught in naïvety, or let yourself should all over things. In other words, don't walk through the wrong part of town in the middle of the night in a neon mini-skirt and a low-cut blouse, and pretend to be surprised if things get uncomfortable.

When you race, accept what racing is, NOT what it should be, and act accordingly.

If someone chops you through a corner, you shouldn't be surprised. If you don't take a pull in a breakaway, expect people to curse you out. As you approach the finish line in a group, you shouldn't be stunned if you feel a hand on your hip or a helmet on your shoulder.

Instead of being surprised, throw it right back and act em'. Give as good as you get. Do you want people to respect your racing line through a corner? Show them what happens if they try to take it.
