I hemmed and hawed about sending this post, but I think I skipped my kid's soccer game to write it, so if I don't publish it, I will miss part of his childhood for nothing.

A few months ago, my athlete sent me this post:

Don't roll your eyes! If your initial instinct was to think this post was cringe, that's understandable, but it's wrong. On the contrary, this post is a masterclass of social media manipulation from a guy who continues to make bank playing the greatest hits to an increasingly less-wowed public.

So, what did he do? He pretended to be hurt/offended because people gave him shit about not being able to bench more than half his body weight. Then, he responded to this criticism/shit by doing another post saying that while his physique won't be on the cover of Men's Health anytime soon, he's really fast, though, especially for his age. While the tradeoff isn't pretty, it's a tradeoff he'll take, all with his shirt off, of course. Let's get into this:

First, no cyclist, especially at his level, is ashamed that they can't bench more than half their body weight—they're thrilled they can't.

The extent to which a cyclist is weak in their upper body is proportional to how committed they are to cycling*—it's a sign of devotion, not ridicule. The same goes for tan lines, emaciation, having a bike worth more than your car, etc.

Next, he says, "This is what a body looks like that's(huh?) spent 20 years with the main pursuit of riding a bike up a hill as fast as I possibly can."

No, it's not. Plenty of people spend lots of time training to ride their bike really fast uphill (and on flats and downhills) but don't look like that. Looking like that is partially genetic, but more than anything, it's a function of diet, which is definitely the case with Phil because he was quite open in his book about how he used to be a 'fat kid' and used cycling to slim down. Moreover, Phil is no dummy, understanding full well that the key to his schtick, both aesthetically and in substance, involves remaining painfully thin - sponsorship money and YouTube views aren't going to go up if he can't take down KOMs anymore.

But there's something else in that sentence that's incredibly disingenuous. Read it again; what do you notice?

"This is what a body looks like that's spent 20 years with the main pursuit of riding a bike up a hill as fast as possible."

He separated himself from his body! Phil didn't ride his bike for 20 years, with the main pursuit of riding a bike up a hill as fast as possible; 'a body' did! Why would he choose to say 'a body' instead of 'Phil' here?

If 'a body' does it, it shifts the agency of the decision to ride his bike uphill for 20 years as fast as possible away from Phil to some other entity, clearing the way for Phil to portray himself as a victim. He didn't make his body look like that; something else did, and thus, we should sympathize with him because poor Phil has to bear the indignity of looking like he just got released from a labor camp (even though that's an aspirational cycling flex). Jesus' cross has nothing on Phil's.

"...But I'm probably top 50 on the planet for my age at the things I care about."

There he is!!!!! That's the Phil two-step I know. He butters us up with a little pity party before smoothly transitioning to telling everyone how big his D is.

Many things struck me about Phil after reading his books. The good? He's:

  1. An incredibly talented cyclist. You don't make the World Tour if you suck.

  2. Arguably a better writer. His books are entertaining, and lots of people buy them.

  3. Hilarious. As long as he can hide behind his pen or phone.

The bad? He's:

  1. Unbelievably money-focused. Some of his book chapters are mostly rants about how little cyclists get paid, which should surprise exactly no one.

  2. Petty. I personally knew some cyclists who swore they'd never talk to Phil again because he used his book partly to settle old scores and talk shit.

  3. Grandiose...He spent a lot of time telling everyone that his climbing ability was the equivalent of having a 12-inch wang.

  4. ...But Self-effacing. When he realized he wouldn't make it (even though he made it?), he left his cycling team because he didn't want to take a spot from a younger rider.

  5. Mean. It's essential for Phil to let everyone know that if HE couldn't make it (with a 12-inch Johnson!), how could YOU? You should just give up!

  6. Hypocritical. Mr. Anti-Doping talked a tough game about dopers unless the doper happened to be his friend.

Back to the Post:

"Maybe my goals change and start to prioritize general health and fitness as I get older, but this is me at almost 39 years old. Not going to say I'm not self-conscious at the beach, but I'm happy with it overall."

What I can't get over in this sentence isn't a word but a number—39 years old. Phil has been retired for almost ten years now and is pushing 40, and the entire punchline of his life remains, 'Look how much better I am than you.'

We know, Phil—we get it. You're really special, and you can ride a bike uphill fast.

I just hope part of the 'maybe my goals change' sentiment above moves away, reminding us how great he is, and instead shifts to using his talent, wit, writing, and showmanship to change the culture for the better.

What's an example? Imagine Phil spending just a tenth of his time peacocking online and doing something to lift up or bring attention to someone other than himself. Does this savvy wordsmith even know the definition of charity?

Oh

As I was writing that last paragraph and gearing up to send the 'post' button on substack, a small voice said, "You should double-check that Phil is just a self-centered tw*t and doesn't do anything for other people because if he once gave a homeless person a dollar, you would look like a complete tool," So I checked. My blood pressure immediately shot through the roof.

Mother of God, Phil, is like a male Mother Teresa. He:

  1. Fights childhood hunger with No Kid Hungry.

  2. Raises money with Phil's Cookie Fondo.

  3. Supports the Sierra Club

  4. Siphons' earnings from YouTube and Cameo go to various charities.

My deeply Scandinavian former English teacher, Grandmother (RIP), used to visit us when I was young and drop cultured expressions and words that I wasn't yet absorbing from my big-picture dinosaur books. One I never forgot was 'hoisted on your own petard.' Have you never heard that one? It's an exceptional thing to have in your ditty bag:

As a reader, I understand that you might not always agree with my turns of phrase, but I challenge you to find another word or phrase in the English language that better captures how badly the entire thesis of this piece exploded in my head. I should cut my losses and stop writing, but much like the Knight in Monty Python, I refuse to accept defeat and allow Phil's philanthropic rap sheet to be the last word.

"Judge a Tree by Its Fruits"

I think Jesus said that. Or Abraham. Or B ddha. I can't remember.

While I could easily point the finger at social media for fostering the sort of environment that not just rewards but indeed demands gratuitous bids for attention, especially if you're an 'influencer,' which naturally distorts and perverts the person doing the influencing because if they don't someone else will, and then they won't get paid, and then at 39 they're going back to living with Mom and Dad, or worse, their friend's couch, it's not actually social media's fault. People have been doing whatever it took to get attention forever.

What's forgotten (or at least what I forget) are two big things;

  1. Everyone wants attention - that's human. While sneering at those who seek it openly is tempting, at least they're honest about it. Those of us (AHEM!) who pretend not to want or need it but obtain it indirectly are ironically probably more conceited than those they judge.

  2. Attention-grabbing bids aren't a dispositive indictment of an attention whore's character - actions are - the fruits, as it were.

So, as much as it hurts to say this, Phil's post isn't a textbook example of narcissistic influencing but open, honest, maybe even admirable personal honesty. Worse, even if it wasn't, it doesn't matter because Phil does so much for others that he unsettles Mother Teresa's legacy.

I really hate growing as a person in public, but I also realize how much vulnerability I'm displaying by sharing my personal journey with you, which is one of the best attention-getting devices around, arguably even more powerful than pretending to be a victim like Phil!

Queen to e7, Phil, checkmate.
